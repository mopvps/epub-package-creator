15 Professional Indemnity policies

There is no absolute requirement under ICE Conditions of Contract for Professional Indemnity insurance. However, for completeness of this text and to cover situations where such insurance is made obligatory by special additional conditions of contract, consideration of how professional negligence risks can be covered by insurance is discussed in this chapter.

Typical provisions within Professional Indemnity policies for consulting engineers

As with all insurance policies, Professional Indemnity policies can differ. Typical provisions are given in Appendix 5. Wording may provide:

‘To indemnify the Insured against any claim for damages for breach of professional duty which may be made against them during the period of insurance due to any negligent act error or omission whenever or wherever the same was or was alleged to have been committed by the Insured or their predecessors in business or any employee of the Insured or their predecessors in business in the conduct of the business.’

The following points should be noted.

  • A claim must be for a breach of professional duty. For example, claims by visitors who have been injured by a fall in the office do not arise from breach of a professional duty. These claims would be covered by a Public Liability policy.
  • The policy is a ‘claims made’ policy; in other words it responds only to claims made during the currency of the policy. The negligent act, error or omission giving rise to the claim may have been committed some years previously but the policy nevertheless applies (assuming the negligent act, error or omission was unknown to the insured at the time a proposal was made for insurance — if it was previously known but not disclosed to the insurers, the insurers may avoid the policy because of the non-disclosure of material fact).

    • Once the policy lapses there is no cover, notwithstanding the fact that a negligent act, error or omission was committed during the currency. The sole test is: was the policy in force at the date of the claim?

  • Cover relates only to a negligent act, error or omission. The wording is important since if liability arises solely under a warranty, fitness-for-purpose or guarantee clause in the contract for professional services, there may not have been a negligent act, error or omission and there would thus be no cover.
  • There is a limit of indemnity up to which insurers will pay claims, but not beyond. This limit is usually an aggregate for all claims in any one policy year.
  • Insurers will pay the costs and expenses incurred with their consent in the defence or settlement of any claim. However, if the claim is ultimately settled at a higher value than the limit of indemnity, the legal costs and expenses are shared in proportion to the claim and the limit of indemnity. Thus if the limit of indemnity is £2 million and the claim is settled for £4 million, the insurers will pay only one-half of the legal costs and expenses.
  • It is important to insure for an adequate limit. Claims often take a long time to settle and upwards of five years is not unusual. The effects of inflation and monetary values applying at the date of settlement which may increase substantially the cost of a claim must be taken into consideration.
  • The ‘business’ will be carefully defined in the policy, and the policy will operate only in respect of those business activities defined. Care is therefore necessary to see that the description is sufficiently wide and covers all the professional duties to be performed.
  • Contractual agreements in connection with consortia and joint ventures are a matter which require particular scrutiny. Insurers should be fully advised of the contractual arrangements and agreement reached as to exactly what cover is necessary in order to obtain full cover of the insured’s risks. A professional firm may find itself carrying the responsibilities of other parties; this can have serious consequences if those other parties are uninsured or go out of business. This should be considered in consultation with the insurers.
  • An annual policy may exclude work outside the United Kingdom, particularly in the United States and Canada. This is because professional liability exposures in overseas countries may differ substantially from liability exposures in the UK. In addition it will be important to establish which system of law will govern any dispute, i.e. English or foreign law. Even if work abroad is covered, it may be subject to a legal jurisdiction clause in the policy, e.g. that claims are only covered if they are madein UK courts. Such jurisdiction clauses must be read with care and their meaning understood.
  • It may be difficult, if not impossible, always to obtain adequate cover for injury, damage or loss of use of property arising from seepage, pollution or contamination (including the costs of removing, neutralising and cleaning up afterwards) which have resulted from the consulting engineer’s activities. Typical provisions in the policy schedule stating limits of indemnity now include additionally a limitation that cover for claims in respect of seepage, pollution or contamination shall notexceed an aggregate amount in any one year. The long-term availability of professional indemnity cover remains in doubt at the time of writing.

Extensions to cover

It is usually possible to extend a basic policy to give additional cover for risks such as the following.

  • Libel or slander.
  • Third parties who have sustained losses arising out of the fraud or dishonesty of employees. Deliberate acts of fraud or dishonesty by employees are not normally covered by the policy, which refers to any ‘negligent act, error or omission’, i.e. some inadvertent mistake. Thus an employee may deliberately give misleading information, destroy plans, records or documents, give false certificates or permit other acts of dishonesty for which his/her employer may be liable. The extension does not cover loss of the employer’s own money or property, which should be the subject of separate Fidelity Guarantee insurance. The policy is intended to cover claims by third parties, not by the insured.
  • Infringement of copyright.
  • Protection to employees in addition to the named Insured. Legally, having settled a claim against the named Insured arising out of an employee’s negligence, the insurers could have rights of subrogation against the employees concerned. An ‘indemnity to employees’ or a ‘waiver of subrogation rights against employees’ clause gives protection to the employee.
  • Loss of documents. This covers liability for the consequences of loss of documents which have been entrusted to or belong to the insured. Besides legal liability, the extension may include reasonable costs in replacing or restoring documents.

Disclosure of all known material facts

As discussed in Chapter 1, before insurers issue a Professional Indemnity policy, they will require the completion of a proposal form asking a number of specific questions. The answers to the questions must be complete and the Insured must not fail to disclose any material facts that may have a bearing on the underwriter’s acceptance of the risk or terms and conditions agreed.

The duty to disclose all material facts arises again at renewal. It is usual for insurers to ask for the completion of a declaration form (or in some cases a new proposal form) at each renewal date of the policy because at that time a new insurance contract comes into existence.

The person signing the proposal form should consult with all relevant persons to make sure that all material facts are disclosed and the proposal form has been completed correctly. Anything which is relevant to professional liability or may give rise to a claim or has a bearing on the Professional Indemnity policy should be identified and if in doubt discussed with the insurers. Failure to do so may render cover invalid.

Conditions of policy

There are a number of important policy conditions which normally must be complied with. Failure to do so may prejudice a policy and insurers can then refuse to indemnify the Insured. It is important to comply with policy conditions such as the following.

  • The Insured must not admit liability or in any other way attempt to negotiate or compromise a claim. All negotiations will be carried out by the insurers unless they give permission for the Insured to do so.
  • To avoid adverse publicity and differences of opinion between the Insured and the insurers, the Insured must not contest any legal proceedings unless advice from a legal representative mutually agreed upon by the Insured and the insurers advises that such proceedings should be contested.
  • The Insured must give immediate notification to the insurers of any claim made, or receipt of any information from any person of an intention to make a claim, against the Insured. This includes the Insured becoming aware of any occurrence which can subsequently give rise to a claim (even though no claim has been made at the time). For example, an Insured may discover a serious mistake in calculations which has not yet given rise to a claim, or he may have reason to believe a design is faulty. Most policies provide that once that occurrence has been reported to the insurers, any subsequent claim which may be made against the Insured arising out of that occurrence (even if many years later) will be deemed to have been made during the currency of the policy in force when the occurrence was notified.

Typical provisions for Professional Indemnity within Contractor’s design and construct contracts

Clause 8(2) of both of ICE 6 and ICE Design and Construct conditions of contract limits a Contractor’s design obligation to ‘all reasonable skill, care and due diligence’. There is no requirement for a Contractor or a Contractor’s designer (whether in-house or from an outside consultancy) to obtain Professional Indemnity insurance. This is a matter for the Contractor and/or consultant to decide, unless an Employer specifically requires compulsory Professional Indemnity insurance to be held by the Contractor, by so stating in an additional clause to the standard conditions of contract.

A Contractor will be liable when his design faults affect the Works, whether there is negligence or not. A typical Professional Indemnity policy will only normally cover ‘negligent acts errors or omissions’. It will not therefore give a Contractor’s design department protection where there is no negligence.

Typical provisions for Professional Indemnity within Contractor’s other policies

Aspects of Contractor’s professional negligence risk may be covered in different Contractor policies.

Contractor’s Professional Indemnity policy

A specimen Contractor’s Professional Indemnity policy is provided in Appendix 6. The following points are briefly noted.

  • Third party risks (i.e. loss of or damage to third party property) may or may not be included in a Contractor’s design department’s Professional Indemnity policy. There would, of course, be no need to include them if they are covered by a Public Liability policy.
  • The Contractor’s Professional Indemnity policy’s main purpose is to provide cover for claims by others for damage to or faults (where those are due to some neglect, error or omission on the part of the Contractor’s design department) in the contract works after practical completion. However, ‘first party’ cover is sometimes included in Contractor’s design and construction Professional Indemnity policies to cover the Contractor’s costs of rectifying faults discovered during the construction period and prior to handover.

Contractor’s Public Liability policy

  • This policy protects a Contractor against legal liability for injury to third parties and physical damage to property of third parties, plus any third party consequential losses which may flow from that injury or damage. It will usually cover the ‘professional’ activities of a Contractor which might give rise to such damage, provided there is no specific policy exclusion of claims due to breach of professional duty or arising from advice, design, plan or specification.
  • It does not cover damage to the contract works: that is the purpose of a Contractor’s Contract Works policy.

Contractor’s Contract Works policy

This policy is primarily to cover damage to the contract works during construction. It will normally exclude damage to the works arising out of design, plan or specification. The exclusion may be modified (refer to DE Clauses as described in Chapter 10) so that it is only a defective part which is excluded. Thus, if the defective part causes damage to non-defective parts, damage to those non-defective parts is covered. Problems arise in practice in defining which is the ‘part’ and what constitutes ‘damage’.
