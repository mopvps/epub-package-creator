10 A Contract Works policy

Contract Works policies vary from insurer to insurer; a typical example is reproduced in Appendix 3. The policy is written on the basis of ‘all risks of loss or damage’. This is a misleading form of shorthand used in the insurance industry and does not mean that every loss or damage is covered by the policy: it does no more than describe the insuring wording in Section 1, which is subject to policy exceptions, terms and conditions.

Section 1 — Damage to the Works

This sets out the circumstances in which damage to the Works is covered.

  • The Insured must have paid or have agreed to pay the premium.
  • The insurer will indemnify the Insured against loss or damage to the Works by repair, reinstatement or replacement.
  • To comply with the requirements of the construction contract the Insured will be the Contractor and the Employer.
  • The indemnity relates to all forms of loss or damage (although a number of exceptions, to be discussed later, limit the cover). Arguments can arise as to whether insurers require the Works to be physically damaged and the meaning of ‘damage’. For example, if a hole is dug and the sides collapse, or it is flooded, is the hole damaged?
  • The property insured is described in detail in a schedule to the policy.
  • The loss or damage must occur during the currency of the policy. It follows that once the policy lapses there is no cover.
  • The loss or damage must occur on sites within Great Britain, Ireland, Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man or the Channel Islands, although in practice the policy can be extended to apply to damage in other parts of the world.
  • The policy is limited to loss or damage on or adjacent to the contract site but a number of extensions can widen this cover, as will be explained in the following section. The Contractor is responsible for loss or damage to goods and materials off the site and while in transit, but the policy can be extended to give this cover. The Contractor may also have a responsibility to insure goods and materials, the ownership of which may have been vested in the Employer under the pertaining construction contract conditions, before delivery to the site.

Section 2 — Extensions

  1. Transit — loss or damage to the Works or materials while in transit — is covered, other than for transport by sea or air (because of the much heavier risks involved). Loss or damage to any mechanically propelled vehicle operating under its own power is not covered because the Contractor should have this risk insured under separate plant or motor policies.

    Employees’ tools and effects are excluded because to include these would result in many small claims which would erode unreasonably the premium paid to the insurer.

  2. By including the Principal (Employer) as an insured party under the contract, the policy complies with any Construction Contract conditions requiring the Contractor to insure the works in the joint names of the Employer and the Contractor.
  3. Extension 3, dealing with Architects’, Surveyors’ and Consulting Engineers’ fees, is self-explanatory. The Insurer will not pay the fees incurred in assisting the Insured to prepare a claim against the Insurers since they see no logic in financing claims against themselves.
  4. Many expensive claims are paid under the extension covering the removal of debris. It is not only the cost of removing debris from the site which is covered but also the cost of removing it to another dumping ground, which may be some distance away. Property remaining on the contract site may have to be shored or propped up.
  5. Materials and goods, while not on the site of the contract but intended for inclusion in the Works and for which the Contractor is responsible, are covered by an extension to the policy while they are stored off-site. Under some standard conditions of contract the ownership of certain goods and materials stored elsewhere but intended for the site is transferred to the Employer. However, the Contractor remains responsible for any loss or damage to them and must, under the conditions of contract, insure them under against damage from ‘any cause’. In fact, notwithstanding any contract requirement, a policy will not cover damage from ‘any cause’: it has limitations and exclusions.

    6. Section 3 — Exceptions

    Exceptions 1 to 13 deal with injury, loss or damage in circumstances where the Insurer is not prepared to pay.

    1. The Insured’s retained liability, e.g. deductibles or excesses, normally appear in policies of this type. They are intended to cut out small claims, and are imposed by insurers where they wish to reduce the effect of an adverse feature of the risk, or to make Contractor take more care.
    2. The object of the policy is to cover the risks of loss or damage to the works during the construction period. Once a contract has been completed and the works have been handed over to the Employer, the risk passes to the Employer, who has responsibility to arrange insurance. Care is necessary to ensure that there is no shortfall in cover during the changeover period of risk from Contractor to Employer.
    3. Once the construction period has finished, the policy ceases to apply except to pick up any liability for loss or damage under insurance contract terms applicable to any Maintenance Period or Defects Liability Period. Once a Certificate of Completion, or equivalent, has been issued, at the end of such period, the remaining risk of loss or damage passes to the Employer.

      Works ‘taken into use by the owner, tenant or occupier’ are excluded. The exclusion may be modified to permit use or occupation, provided all the facts are disclosed to the Insurer and the appropriate premium is paid.

    4. There are six items contained in this exception.
      1. (a) Damage to any existing structure is not covered because it is not part of the Contract Works. Borderline cases may arise: then there is nothing to stop an agreement being reached with the insurers to regard such existing property as part of the Contract Works for the purposes of the policy and to have the sum insured and, if appropriate, the premium increased accordingly. Thus damage to existing property will be covered on an ‘all risks’ basis, regardless of fault.

        Normally, however, the position is that if a Contractor damages existing property while carrying out the Works, and this is caused by the Contractor’s negligence or where the Contractor is liable under a Construction Contract condition, the Employer can sue the Contractor, who should be able to recover under a Public Liability policy.

      2. (b) Deeds, bonds, bills of exchange etc. are excluded because these risks should be separately insured under a Money policy; in any case it is doubtful whether the items could be considered to be part of the Permanent or Temporary Works.
      3. (c) This item removes the mechanical vehicle risk from the policy because the Contractor should have these risks insured under a separate Motor or Plant policy. If an item of plant is used solely as a tool of trade on-site, then the policy applies.
      4. (d) Despite the fact that Construction Contract conditions may require it to be insured, damage to Contractor’s constructional plant due to its own breakdown is excluded because this should be covered by a Machinery Breakdown insurance. Such insurance is expensive and, since constructional plant may be subject to rapid deterioration and damage unless subject to regular maintenance, it is not available unless the Insurer can be satisfied as to the Contractor’s maintenance arrangements.
      5. (e) Vessels or craft made to float in or on water are excluded because these can be insured more adequately by Marine insurance.
      6. (f) This exception, dealing with property for which the Contractor is relieved of responsibility by Construction Contract conditions, is important. Thus, even though an Employer is insured as a named party under the policy, the Employer will have no protection in respect of loss or damage arising from Excepted Risks. Many of these are not insurable by the commercial insurance market. However, damage arising out of use or occupation by the Employer, his agents or servants or other contractors, is a risk which the Insurer may be prepared to include in the policy if full details are made available. The same may be true of loss or damage arising from fault, defect, error or omission in the design of the Works, a subject which will be mentioned in greater detail later. Unless consideration is given to providing an Employer with some measure of cover for these Excepted Risks at the time the insurance is arranged, the Employer remains uninsured unless independent arrangements are made elsewhere.
    5. The exception contains five alternative Defect Exclusion (DE) clauses which are known as DE1 to DE5. These were published by the Association of British Insurers and adopted in 1995 by most of the insurers issuing CAR policies in the UK. They are provisions regarded as acceptable for use in CAR policies. A brief description of their effect is given below.
      • DE1 — Outright Defect Exclusion

        • Insurers will not pay for loss or damage caused by specified defects; such matters are regarded as trade risks. It will be remembered that there must first be an instance of loss or damage before the policy operates at all; it is therefore unnecessary to exclude the cost of repairing defects.

      • DE2 — Extended Defective Condition Exclusion

        • The exclusion is limited to that which is defective and the property relying for support or stability on the defective Works. Other Works which are not defective, but are damaged, are insured. An example is a steel-framed building with the steel members bolted together, surrounded by a dwarf wall. A bolt securing roof members is defective so that the building collapses and destroys the dwarf wall. DE2 would exclude the entire steel frame (as relying for support on the defective bolt) but would leave the dwarf wall within cover.

      • DE3 — Limited Defective Condition Exclusion

        • This is similar to DE2 but there is no exclusion in respect of works which rely upon defective Works for support or stability. Applying DE3 to the previous example would remove from cover the roof section of the building (which is defective) but would leave the other damage within cover.

      • DE4 — Defective Part Exclusion

        • Here the exclusion is limited to only the defective part or component and not the Works of which it is a part. In the foregoing example DE4 would be effective to remove only the defective bolt from cover.

      • DE5 — Design Improvement Exclusion

        • The cover is much wider: in the event of damage, the policy will pay for repairs. Only the cost of improvement is uninsured. In the example the entire loss would be insured except for improvements such as an upgrade in the bolt specification for increased strength.

      The extent to which an insurer will be prepared to insure on the basis of DE4 or DE5 is uncertain. While it is a matter of negotiation in each particular case, it is unlikely that contractors other than the major UK civil engineering companies, with a proven track record, will be able to insure on those terms.

      Whether there is a ‘defect’ should be decided first and independently of any question of fault, or negligence on the part of the designer. In Hitchens Hatfield v. Prudential (1993) the court decided that while the phrase ‘defectively designed’ in a policy exclusion required proof of fault, the phrase ‘defective in design’ did not. The question is simply: ‘Is there a defect?’ If that question is answered in the affirmative, the exclusion will apply.

    6. The cost of normal upkeep or normal making good.
    7. Damage by wear, tear, rust and mildew is excluded because insurers again regard it as a trade risk that is not insurable. The exclusion is usually qualified to make it clear that other parts of the Works which are free from such defects, but are damaged as a result of them, are covered.
    8. Loss or damage to property only discovered when an inventory is made is excluded, because insurers only intend to pay for loss or damage which can be traced to an identifiable cause or event. They can then investigate that event to see whether it is within the terms of the policy. To identify a loss only by an inventory does not define the causative event and prevents the Insurer from applying policy terms and conditions such as policy excess and, in particular, whether more than one excess applies.
    9. Delay, non-completion or consequential loss or damage of any kind is excluded because the object of the policy is to cover physical loss or damage to the Works, not loss consequent upon delay. Losses incurred by the Employer, e.g. loss of income or profit because of late completion following damage, may be covered under a separate policy, usually called ‘Advance loss of profits’ (ALOP) policy. A Contractor may also be able to insure against some of the consequences of delay, e.g. liquidated damages, under a separatge policy but not in the CAR insurance market.
    10. —13. The remaining exceptions 10, 11, 12 and 13 deal with exclusions which are found in all insurance policies and relate to risks against which there is no commercial insurance available.

Other exceptions

Because insurance policies are not standardised there may be additional exceptions which appear in addition to those shown in the specimen policy in Appendix 3. For example, other policies may exclude loss or damage arising out of cessation of work arising, for instance, where there is a strike or labour dispute or a problem with suppliers or delays in deliveries; as a consequence a site may be left unattended or with a minimum of security. This would represent an increase in risk, which is why the exception would appear. Another example is where a contract includes the installation or erection of plant or machinery which has to be tested or commissioned before the Employer will accept it. Testing and commissioning may be excluded on the basis that such activities represent high risks, particularly those relating to breakdown, fire or explosion. The risks may be included subject to special negotiation with insurers.

Section 4 — Limits of liability

The sums insured on the Works, Plant and other property are set out in the typical policy schedule in Section 7.

The final paragraph of Section 4 allows the sum insured to be reinstated, should there be a claim. Thus if property is insured for £2 million and there is a claim for £1 million, the sum insured is not reduced. It automatically reverts to £2 million. An additional premium is payable.

Section 5 — General conditions

Like all insurance policies there are conditions which have to be complied with before an insured is entitled to the benefit of the policy.

  1. This is a standard condition in mjost policies and is self-explanatory.

    2. Take care

  2. An Insured is required to take all reasonable precautions to prevent loss or damage, since insurance is not intended to be a substitute for good management and supervision. The theory is that the Insured should proceed in exactly the same way whether or not he is insured. Insurance is not an excuse for taking short cuts — although in practice, of course, this happens.

    3. Should a Contractor fail to take reasonable precautions to prevent loss or damage, however, he runs the risk of breaching the terms of the policy. The Insurer would, however, have to prove recklessness on the part of a director or senior manager to establish a breach of the condition in the same way as in respect of the similar condition in the Public Liability policy.

    Change in risk

  3. This condition requires any change in the nature of the risk to be notified to the insurers, and the Insured in the meantime must take such additional precautions as are necessary. It represents an onerous condition and the Insured must be careful to remember it and comply with it. It is there because the premium and risk assessment are calculated on the facts known to the Insurer. If the risk changes, the Insurer needs to know. It may affect the premium, the assessment of the risk and how much of the risk the Insurer decides to carry. Insurers often reinsure parts of their risk to keep their own exposure within reasonable boundaries.

    4. Observing terms of policy

  4. This is again a typical condition which makes the original proposal for insurance the basis for the insurance contract, so that if anything is wrong or inaccurate in the proposal the policy is voidable.

    The condition makes observance of policy terms and conditions a condition precedent, so that the insured has to comply with the terms and conditions of the policy before there is entitlement to indemnity.

    5. Premium adjustment

  5. This condition is a typical clause dealing with the adjustment of premium. Most premiums are calculated by applying a rate per cent to contract values at the beginning of the year and at the end of the year the insured declares the correct values when they are known. An adjustment of premium is then made and the insured is either entitled to a return of premium or charged an additional premium.

Section 6 — Claims conditions

These conditions should be read carefully by an Insured. For example, in the event of some event occurring which may give rise to a claim, the Insurer has to be informed in writing with full details. This is to enable investigation of the occurrence while the facts are still fresh in the minds of all the people concerned.

Notification of the police is necessary in the case of theft, loss or wilful damage. If loss or damage due to these causes is not notified to the insurers within three calendar months, there is no cover under the policy.

Condition 5 allows arbitration if there is a dispute as to the amount to be paid under the policy. It does not arise where there is a dispute relating to the words of the policy itself. Historically, Contractors have preferred to have disputes about wording dealt with by the courts. Insurers, therefore, have restricted their insurance contract arbitration clauses to matters of quantum only.

All the terms and conditions of a policy should be understood and complied with. In practice the terms and conditions of the policy are frequently breached by an Insured, but insurers nevertheless will normally deal reasonably with most claims.

Section 7 — The schedule

This is largely self-explanatory, but some points require special attention.

The Insured will normally be shown as the Contractor (particularly if reliance is placed on an annual policy which has to cover more than one contract). To comply with the ICE Conditions of Contract an Employer obtains indemnity under the heading ‘Indemnity to Principals’ discussed earlier, which for practical reasons insurers regard as tantamount to insurance in the joint names.

Where a single policy is being issued to cover the Works (rather than relying on a Contractor’s annual policy), then the Insured would be shown as the Contractor, the Employer and other interested parties such as sub-contractors and financiers.

The period of insurance must cover the full period of the contract. Where the Contractor is relying on an annual policy, it must be renewed each year.

The business should be correctly described to cover all the activities, a point which has been mentioned several times previously.

The description of the Contract Works insured is self-explanatory and includes free-issue materials. It is important for the Contractor to ensure that the sums insured are correct and are sufficient to provide enough money to replace any work lost or damaged. To select the tender price is dangerous, as variations to the contract plus inflation during the period of the contract may make this figure inadequate. As the contract moves towards completion there is a build-up of risk; what is required is a sum of money to enable the Contractor to recomplete the contract, should there be a total loss. This could include demolition and debris removal as well as rebuilding. The policy does provide for some inflation by stipulating that the sum insured should not exceed 120% of the estimated original tendered contract price, including the value of free materials.
