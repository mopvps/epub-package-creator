2 The basis of legal liability for personal injury or damage to third party property

Accidents on construction projects are not uncommon. People are injured or their property is damaged. Those people, however, have no automatic right to obtain damages from those who caused the injury or damage. They must first prove legal liability (fault).

How liability arises

There are several ways in which legal liability may attach to the defendant. They include:

  • negligence;
  • nuisance;
  • trespass;
  • breach of statutory duty;
  • strict liability;
  • liability under contract; and
  • vicarious liability.

Other than liability under contract, these forms of liability are referred to as torts, i.e. civil wrongs independent of contract. This Chapter describes some of the legal issues involved in order to understand how liability arises, how it may be excluded, modified, apportioned or transferred under appropriate clauses in a building or engineering contract and the extent of cover available under legal liability insurance policies.

Negligence

Negligence produces most claims. Negligence is omitting to do something which a reasonable and prudent person would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable person would not do. Negligence on its own, however, will not bring liability. The sole occupant of an island can be as negligent as he wishes since there is no other person at risk. It is only when other persons or property are at risk that there is a duty of care not to be negligent.

To succeed in an action for negligence a plaintiff must prove:

  • that the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care;
  • that the defendant breached that duty of care; and
  • that the plaintiff suffered injury or damage as a result of the breach.

Duty of care

A defendant owes a duty of care to those whom he ought reasonably to have in mind at the time of carrying out activities. This is the ‘neighbour’ principle, established in the case of Donoghue v. Stephenson (1932), where Lord Atkin answering the question ‘Who in law is my neighbour?’ said, ‘Persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so affected when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question’.

Whether there is a duty of care at any particular time is a question of law to be decided by the court.

Reasonable care

The extent of the duty of care depends upon reasonable foresight and reasonable care. A defendant will owe a duty of care to those persons whom he can reasonably foresee may be injured by his acts or omissions but the defendant will only breach a duty of care if, reasonably foreseeing the risk of loss or damage to a neighbour, he does not take reasonable care to avoid it. For instance, if a defendant knows of loose tiles on a roof of his own property, he owes a duty of care to prevent them falling and causing third party injury or damage. The standard of ‘reasonable care’ is an objective one, being that of the ordinary reasonable person in the circumstances of the case.

The burden of proving negligence rests upon the plaintiff, except where the principle of res ipsa loquitur (the thing speaks of itself) applies. If a sack of flour falls out of a building on to an innocent bystander res ipsa loquitur applies. In the natural order of things, sacks of flour ought not to fall out of buildings. Negligence is therefore presumed against the defendant, to whom the onus of disproving it passes. The defendant may rebut the presumption by showing that the accident occurred without any negligence on his part.

Even though a person may be negligent, a plaintiff will not succeed if his injury or damage was too remote, i.e. if there is not a sufficient causal link between the act of negligence and the injury, loss or damage suffered. For example, a motorist who injures a child on holiday will not generally be liable for the shock to the mother at home or when she is told the news several days later. From the defendant’s point of view the injury to the mother is too remote; it is too far removed from the act of negligence to create liability.

Further, a plaintiff will not succeed with a claim for ‘pure economic loss’, which is a loss which does not arise from physical damage to the plaintiffs tangible property; for example, a plaintiff cannot claim in negligence for an undamaged building that will not carry its expected loadings due to an error or omission of the defendant. This does not apply where the defendant is guilty of a negligent mis-statement. In Hedley Byrne v. Heller & Partners (1963) the House of Lords decided that where a statement is made by one party to another and there is a ‘special relationship’ between the parties, the maker of the statement owes a duty to act with reasonable skill and care. The ‘special relationship’ is a combination of one party having knowledge which the other does not and the reliance of the other upon the mis-statement.

Nuisance

Nuisance is a wrong done to someone by creating noise, smells, dust or vibrations, thus unlawfully disturbing that person in the enjoyment of property or, in some cases, in the exercise of a right such as a right of way or a right to light. The basic principle of the law relating to nuisance is that a person must use property in a way that does not cause harm to others.

A public nuisance is a crime. A nuisance will not be a public one unless the number of persons affected is substantially large enough to constitute a ‘class’ of persons, e.g. a large number of people affected by blasting and quarrying operations near their homes. A public nuisance is only actionable as a civil claim if a plaintiff can show that particular damage was suffered over and above that suffered by the rest of the public at large.

A private nuisance is an interference over a period of time by owners or occupiers of property with the use and enjoyment by others of neighbouring property. A private nuisance can consist of allowing the escape of noxious things such as smoke, smells, noise, gas, vibration, damp and so on, so as to interfere with the health, comfort or convenience of neighbours in the enjoyment of their property, or the wrongful disturbance of rights attached to land, such as a right to light and air, rights to support of land and buildings and rights in respect of water and rivers. Liability for such disturbances of rights attaching to land is strict, i.e. without proof of negligence.

Nuisance and negligence may overlap. Although some actions in nuisance arise from intentional acts, some also arise from a defendant’s inadvertence and in these cases the plaintiff may sue in nuisance or negligence.

Trespass

Trespass is an unlawful act committed on the person, property or right of another. The essence of trespass is the direct injury. Indirect injuries fall within the areas of negligence or nuisance. Trespass is a strict tort and is actionable per se, that is without proof of special damage (particular financial loss). It is a trespass for a contractor to go on to another person’s land without lawful authority, even if he causes no damage; the injury is the entry on to the land without permission or lawful excuse.

Trespass may be of three kinds — to land, to goods and to the person.

Trespass to land

An unlawful entry on to land or buildings in the possession of another is trespass. If a person remains on the land or allows things he has unlawfully placed on the land to remain there, such trespass can be the subject of a fresh action for each day that it continues. Trespass in such circumstances is a continuing trespass. A possessor of land possesses the soil beneath and the claim of air above it. As a consequence, an occupier may remove things trespassing into the overlying air space even though they do no harm, e.g. overhanging branches or tower cranes. Trespass may also be restrained by injunction; for obvious reasons those engaged in civil engineering must take care that the works do not encroach on to the land of others.

Trespass to goods

Trespass to goods consists of a direct and unlawful injury to or interference with goods in the possession of another.

The point about possession is essential because it is the sole basis of the plaintiffs right. Only the person in possession of the goods at the time of the trespass can bring the action.

Trespass to a person

Trespass to a person consists of assault or battery or false imprisonment. Battery is the application of unlawful force to the person of another. Assault is putting a person in reasonable fear of immediate battery.

Breach of statutory duty

Acts of Parliament often contain regulations designed to prevent injury to persons or damage to property, or to give to persons the legal right to sue for a breach of the regulations contained in the legislation. Examples are requirements of the Factories Acts, the Construction Regulations, the Health & Safety at Work etc. Act, the Building Regulations, the Occupier’s Liability Act, the Fatal Accidents Act and the Mines & Quarries Acts. Although such legislation may lay down criminal sanctions (e.g. fines), a breach of any duty by the defendant under such legislation may be actionable in civil law by the person who is injured., The success of the claim will depend on the construction of the words of the statute. Thus if there is a statutory duty to fence dangerous machinery, a failure to fence will be a breach of the duty and the breach may be absolute. Alternatively, legislation may require fencing ‘in so far as it is reasonable or practical to do so’. This is not absolute and an investigation would need to be made into whether the fencing at the material time was reasonable or practicable. In other words, the risk has to be balanced against the practicability of the measures needed to avoid it. Cost, for example, has to be taken into account.

The burden of proof that there has been a breach of statutory duty is upon the plaintiff. The standard of proof required in civil actions is proof upon a balance of probability. In addition, a plaintiff must prove:

  • that a breach of the duty caused injury, in other words that there was a causal link. For example, a steel erector fell and was killed because he was not wearing a safety belt in accordance with statutory regulations. It was the practice of steel erectors never to wear belts; thus if belts had been available it was improbable that the deceased would have worn one. The claim failed, as the breach of duty did not cause the accident.
  • that the plaintiff was one of a class of persons the statute was intended to protect. For example, an engine driver was injured when a car ran into a level crossing gate which had not been maintained in accordance with the statutory requirements. His claim failed because the purpose of the Act was to protect road users, not engine drivers.
  • that the injury of which the plaintiff complains is one the statute was intended to prevent. For example, a plaintiff’s sheep were washed overboard because the defendant had failed to provide pens on the ship’s deck as required by statute. The claim failed. The object of the statute was to prevent the spread of disease among sheep, not to prevent them from being washed overboard.

Negligence and breach of statutory duty are usually regarded as two distinct claims even though there may be some overlapping – for example, where the statutory duty is to ‘take all such circumstances as in the case are reasonable’. In negligence the standard of care is decided by the court, whereas in breach of statutory duty the standard of care is actually laid down in the statute.

Strict liability

A defendant may have liabilities imposed by law which cannot be avoided even with the exercise of reasonable care and skill. The liability is said therefore to be strict, since it does not require proof of negligence or lack of care or wrongful intention. Strict liability can occur in the cirumstances such as those covered by the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868) (liability for escape of dangerous things on defendant’s land). In that case, an employer engaged an independent contractor to construct a reservoir on the employer’s land. Unknown to the employer, there were disused mine shafts on the site and when the reservoir was filled the plaintiffs adjoining coal mine was flooded. The employer was held liable since the court held that ‘any person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it at his peril and if he does not do so it is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequences of its escape’.

Whether a ‘thing’ which has been brought on to the defendant’s land is one which is likely to do mischief if it escapes is a question of fact. A wide variety of things have been held to be within the Rylands v. Fletcher rule, such as electricity, gas, sewage and vibration.

For the rule to apply there must be an escape from the ‘defendant’s land of the ‘thing likely to do mischief’ and there must have been a non-natural use of the defendant’s land. In one case the defendant had explosives on his land. The plaintiff, a munitions inspector of the Ministry of Supply, was injured, while on the premises, by an explosion. He had no claim under the Rylands v. Fletcher rule since his injuries had taken place on the land. There had been no escape from the land of the dangerous thing. Also there must be a non-natural use of the land, so things naturally on the land, such as weeds, are not within the rule.

Animals

The Animals Act 1971 codified the previous common law relating to animals.

There is a strict liability for injury caused by dangerous animals (defined as fully grown animals which, unless restrained, are likely to cause severe damage). The liability is imposed on the ‘keeper’, i.e. the owner or the person who had the animal in his possession.

Guard dogs on contract sites could come within the rule. Trespass by cattle may also be strict.

Fire

Strict liability may attach to certain forms of damage arising out of fire, and liability for fire is often governed by the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher or by statute.

An employer is liable for fire damage caused by the negligence of his independent contractors. In Balfour v. Barty-King (1956), the occupier of a house engaged contractors to thaw her water pipes. They used blowlamps and set fire to the house. The fire spread to the neighbouring house, causing damage. The occupier was held liable.

Product liability

The Consumer Protection Act 1987 introduced strict liability on producers of goods which are defective, causing personal injury or damage to personal property.

Liability under contract

Liability under the torts considered previously is a liability imposed upon the parties by law. Under the terms and conditions of written contracts, liabilities are imposed upon or accepted by the parties voluntarily by the terms of that contract. Thus parties may add to or detract from their liability in tort. Such contracts often contain indemnity clauses of the types seen in the ICE Conditions of Contract, where the liability of the parties may be widened.

Vicarious liability

An employer has vicarious liability for the acts or omissions of employees and, in some circumstances, for his/her independent contractors. First of all, it is necessary to recognise that there is a distinction in law between an employee, who is a person employed under a contract of service, and an independent contractor, who is a person employed under a contract for services.

An independent contractor is a person engaged by the employer to produce a given result where the employer has no power to direct the method of doing the work. This is left to the discretion of the independent contractor, who has the necessary skills. On the other hand, an employee is usually subject to the control of the employer and must follow instructions.

An employer is normally vicariously liable for the activities of employees because the employer has the power of control over them and thus will be liable for their acts or omissions during the course of their employment. The employer is not, as a matter of general principle, liable for the activities of independent contractors since normally the control factor is missing. There are, however, important exceptions to this principle and the employer may be liable for the acts of independent contractors if the liability arises from the work itself. In these circumstances the employer is under a strict duty to avoid injury or damage. The following examples illustrate such circumstances.

  • Where the work involved ‘extra-hazardous’ operations. The defendant was engaged by the plaintiff to take flashlight photographs in a third party cinema. The defendant negligently caused fire for which the plaintiff had to compensate the cinema owner. The court held that the plaintiff could recover this amount from the defendant because the plaintiff was liable to the cinema owner for the independent contractor’s extra-hazardous act.

    • Work in connection with the withdrawal of support to land or buildings would come under the ‘extra-hazardous work’ definition. So too would work which is being performed on or close to a highway, e.g. a demolition contract.

  • Where a contractor is employed to do illegal or unlawful work. The defendant engaged a contractor to dig trenches in the street, without obtaining the necessary authority. The plaintiff fell over stones left by the contractor and the defendant was held liable.
  • Where a measure of control over a contractor is retained by an employer through the provision of machinery or men. Subcontractors were used to discharge ships. The employer provided equipment to enable them to do this. People engaged by the sub-contractors were injured. The employer was held liable because of the employer’s retention of a sufficient degree of control over the workmen by the provision of the equipment.
  • Where an employer is under a statutory duty to perform work in a particular way. The defendants were engaged by an employer to construct a bridge across a river but were restricted by statute to a requirement that the bridge should not detain any vessel using the river longer than was necessary. The bridge could not be opened for several days because the independent contractor did not do the job properly. The employer was held liable.
  • Where the negligence is that of an employer using an inexperienced or incompetent contractor or failing to give sufficient instructions. An employer was held responsible for the activities of a contractor who had deposited sewage from a cleaning contract on to another person’s land. It was adjudged that the employer should have taken more care to see that the contractor disposed of the sewage correctly.

For an employer to be liable, the tortious activities of an independent contractor must be ones upon which the contractor is employed to work and not merely ancillary acts connected with it. In other words, an employer is not liable for what is known as the ‘collateral negligence’ of an independent contractor. In one case the defendant was a main contractor. A sub-contractor injured the plaintiff by carelessly leaving a tool on a window sill from which it fell on to the plaintiff. The court held that the main contractor could not be held liable for the simple collateral negligence of the sub-contractor.

Defences

Several defences to a claim may be open to a defendant; these are as follows.

Denial

The obvious one is that of denial, i.e. the defendant denies that, on the facts, the acts or omissions complained of amount to negligence or breach of any duty owed to the plaintiff.

Inevitable accident

It is a defence to some torts that an accident has taken place despite the exercise of all reasonable care on the part of the defendant. If the defence is that the accident was inevitable, it is another way of saying that the defendant has not failed in any duty to observe the standards of care expected of him. Thus a contractor may blame the employer because the accident was the inevitable result of undertaking the works for the employer.

Act of God

This is a defence limited to torts involving strict liability, such as the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher. An Act of God is an event that is due to natural consequences directly and exclusively without human intervention and that could not have been prevented by any amount of foresight and care. (Incidentally the view that insurance policies do not cover Acts of God is wrong. They do.)

Emergency action

A defence may be that actions were taken in an emergency and were therefore reasonable in all the circumstances – as, for example, where a motorist swerves to avoid a young child running into the road.

Contributory negligence

Where a plaintiff is partly to blame for an injury or damage, the recoverable damages will be scaled down accordingly; for example, if a plaintiff is 30% to blame, damages are reduced by 30%.

Volenti non fit injuria

A literal translation is ‘to him who is willing there can be no injury’. In other words, a person consenting to undergo a risk cannot later sue if, as a consequence of running the risk, injury or damage was sustained. The consent, however, must be real. A person cannot be taken to have consented to a risk if the nature of that risk was not really understood, e.g. a boxer accepts the risk of injury but not if his opponent has weights in his gloves.

Limitation of action

There are time limits for bringing claims prescribed by legislation. If the claim is not brought within the appropriate time limit, then the defendant may argue that the claim is time-barred.

Statutory authority

Where a statute specifically authorises a certain act to be done, injury or damage arising out of the performance of that act may carry with it statutory exemption from liability. For example, in the early days of steam locomotives the railway companies were exempted from liability for fires caused by escaping sparks. It is a question of interpretation of the words of the appropriate statute as to whether it gives total exemption or partial exemption, i.e. so as not to cover the negligent performance of the statutory function.

Act of a stranger

The true cause of injury of damage may not be that of the defendant’s but of a third party over whom the defendant has no control. For example, a third party discharged water into the defendant’s reservoir so that it overflowed and damaged a plaintiff’s property. The court held that the defendant was not liable, under the principle of Rylands v. Fletcher.

Conclusions

This brief review of the basis of liability is very much an oversimplification, but it shows that there is a complex system of legal rules involved in deciding liability and the extent to which damages will be paid.

The extent to which contracting parties may agree between themselves how such liability will be handled and apportioned is determined by appropriate clauses in the construction contract. This is considered in the next chapter.

The fact that a person may be insured does not affect liability. Insurance cannot change the law, it may merely provide money to meet some or all of the financial consequences of liability. The extent (if any) of the insured’s legal liability has to be investigated and determined, if necessary by the courts. If there is no legal liability, then there is nothing against which an insurer needs to indemnify an insured. If there is legal liability, the insurer will pay the claim on behalf of the insured up to the limit of indemnity provided by the policy.
