11 vidence of insurances, failure to insure and other clauses within ICE Conditions of Contract

Where there is a contractual requirement to insure, and particularly where a ‘joint names’ policy has been arranged under which a second party may directly benefit, either party should be able to check the extent of the cover and also to make sure that the policy contains no limitations or exclusions which leave that party unacceptably unprotected.

For Contractor-arranged insurance, for example, a Contractor should be required to produce to an Employer evidence of insurance and payment of premium. Where a Contractor has more than one policy, e.g. a policy covering, say, the first £5 million of the risk and further policies ‘topping up’ this limit to a higher figure, say £20million, all policies should be mentioned. It must be noted that project-specific policies take some time to prepare and may only be available some months after Works have commenced. Reliance may have to be placed initially on summary information from brokers. Evidence of annual policies should be more readily and more quickly available. Parties should call for and examine policies for two main reasons. First, any indemnity given by one Party to another against claims for injury or damage is worthless unless the first Party has the resources to back up the indemnity: if that Party is inadequately insured, alternative financial resources to do this may be lacking. Secondly, since a second Party is treated as an insured person under a joint policy, that Party has the right, if he so wishes, to claim on the policy for indemnity in the event of loss or damage. The second Party thus needs to be assured as to the adequacy of cover.

The following sections describe the requirements of the various ICE Conditions of Contract.

ICE 6, Clause 25

Wording of Clause 25(1)— Evidence and terms of insurance

‘The Contractor shall provide satisfactory evidence to the Employer prior to the Commencement Date that the insurances required under the Contract have been effected and shall if so required produce the insurance policies for inspection. The terms of all such insurances shall be subject to the approval of the Employer (which approval shall not unreasonably be withheld). The Contractor shall upon request produce to the Employer receipts for the payment of current insurance premiums.’

Commentary on Clause 25(1)

A Contractor has to arrange joint name insurances against loss or damage to the Works (i.e. the Temporary and Permanent Works but excluding Contractor’s Equipment (see Clause 21) and for third party public liability (see Clause 23)). In both cases Clause 25(1) requires the terms of the policies to be approved by the Employer. When required to do so by the Employer, the Contractor must produce the policies prior to the Commencement Date, and receipts showing payment of the premiums.

Wording of Clause 25(2)— Excesses

‘Any excesses on the policies insurance effected under Clauses 21 and 23 shall be as stated by the Contractor in the Appendix to the Form of Tender.’

Commentary on Clause 25(2)

An excess is an amount an insured must pay before drawing on an insurer’s indemnity. As a general rule the higher the excess, the cheaper the premium. An Employer will therefore want to know how much cover is provided by insurance and how much risk is being retained directly by the Contractor. An excess is therefore one of the terms which is subject to the approval of an Employer in accordance with Clause 25(1).

Wording of Clause 25(3)— Remedy on Contractor’s failure to insure

‘If the Contractor fails upon request to produce to the Employer satisfactory evidence that there is in force any of the insurances required under the Contract then the Employer may effect and keep in force any such insurance and pay such premium or premiums as may be necessary for that purpose and from timeto time deduct the amount so paid from any monies due to which may become due to the Contractor or recover the same as a debt due from the Contractor.’

Commentary on Clause 25(3)

Clause 25(3) says that if a Contractor shall fail on request to produce to an Employer ‘satisfactory evidence’ that contractually required insurances are in force, the Employer may take out such insurances and deduct the premiums from amounts due to the Contractor. The Clause refers to ‘satisfactory evidence’ that the insurances are in force. The best evidence is the policies themselves or certified copies of them. That is the only way an Employer can fully know the terms and conditions. The Contractor or his insurers or brokers will usually initially only be able to produce certificates or letters to the effect that certaininsurances are in force and for what period. These certificates or letters are never satisfactory by themselves since they usually include qualifications such as that the insurance is ‘subject otherwise to the terms exceptions limitations and conditions of the policy’. This is of little use to an Employer since it is those terms, exceptions, limits and conditions which may severely limit the cover and to which he would have objected, had he known them. (However, if the certificates or letters stated that an insurance complied with the Contractor’s obligations— without making such a qualification— this could be untrue. Those issuing the certificates or letters would then be exposed to claims for damages for negligent mis-statement if an Employer who placed reliance upon them sustained a loss because the insurances were inadequate.)

Should the Contractor fail to produce ‘satisfactory evidence’ of insurance, there are practical problems for an Employer attempting to arrange equivalent joint names insurances himself,as allowed by Clause 25. Before insurers issue policies, they require much information to enable them to assess the risk and the premium. This would include, for example, details of how long a Contractor has been in business, his experience of the type of work involved, previous insurances held by him and any claims paid under those policies for the preceding five years, and how much they cost. Such knowledge may not be available to the Employer who would, therefore, have difficulty himself in effecting the joint names insurances required. However, the Employer can arrange insurances in his own name protecting his own interest in the works and covering his own legal liability risk, and can charge that premium to the Contractor.

Similarly, should an excess proposed by a Contractor prove unacceptable to an Employer as one of the ‘terms’ of a policy according to Clause 25(1), the Employer can obtain extra cover for that excess and charge the Contractor for that cover under Clause 25(3).

Wording of Clause 25(4)— Compliance with policy conditions

‘Both the Employer and the Contractor shall comply with all conditions laid down in the insurance policies. In the event that the Contractor or the Employer fails to comply with any condition imposed by the insurance policies effected pursuant to the Contract each shall indemnify the other against all losses and claims arising from such failure.’

Commentary on Clause 25(4)

This Clause means that should either party not fulfil the requirements of a policy (e.g. by not giving proper and timely notification of a claim) leading to a loss being incurred because of non-payment by insurers, the non-performing party must accept the financial consequences applicable to the other party.

ICE Design and Construct,Clause 25

Although there are very minor changes in wording, Clause 25 of ICE Design and Construct is essentially the same as that of ICE 6.

ICE Minor Works,Clauses 10.6 and 10.7

Wording of Clause 10.6— Employer to approve insurance

‘⋯ insurance shall be effected with an insurer and in terms approved by the Employer (which approval shall not be unreasonably withheld) for at least the amount stated in the Appendix.’

Wording of Clause 10.7— Contractor to produce policies of insurance

‘Both the Employer and the Contractor shall comply with the terms of any policy issued in connection with the Contract and shall whenever required produce to the Employer the policy or policies of insurance and the receipts for the payments of the current premiums.’

Commentary on Clauses 10.6 and 10.7

The following points will be noted in comparison with ICE 6.

  • There is no specific requirement to produce satisfactory evidence of insurance prior to a commencement date.
  • There is no requirement for a Contractor to state insurance excesses. There is, however, a requirement that terms are subject to approval by the Employer. If an Employer is unhappy with the excess indicated by scrutiny of insurance terms and conditions, he can withhold approval.
  • There is no clause covering a failure of a Contractor to comply with the terms of any policy or even to insure. However, such failure would be a breach of contract (as would a failure to obtain insurance satisfactory to the Employer, including level of excess). An Employer paying for cover himself would be entitled in litigation to recover as damages any costs incurred as a result of that breach.

NEC, Clause 85 — Insurance policies

Wording of Clause 85.1

‘The Contractor submits policies and certificates for the insurance which he is to provide to the Project Manager for acceptance before the starting date and afterwards as the Project Manager instructs. A reason for not accepting the policies and certificates is that they do not comply with this contract.’

Wording of Clause 85.2

‘Insurance policies include a waiver by the insurers of their subrogation rights against directors and other employees of every insured except where there is fraud.’

Wording of Clause 85.3

‘The Parties comply with the terms and conditions of the insurance policies.’

Wording of Clause 85.4

‘Any amount not recovered from an insurer is borne by the mployer for events which are at his risk and by the Contractor for events which are at his risk.’

NEC, Clause 86— If the Contractor does not insure

Wording of Clause 86.1

‘The Employer may insure a risk which this contract requires the Contractor to insure if the Contractor does not submit a required policy or certificate. The cost of this insurance to the Employer is paid by the Contractor.’

NEC, Clause 87— Insurance by the Employer

Wording of Clause 87.1

‘The Project Manager submits policies and certificates for insurances provided by the Employer to the Contractor for acceptance before the starting date and afterwards as the Contractor instructs. The Contractor accepts the policies and certificates if they comply with this contract. ’

Wording of Clause 87.2

‘The Contractor’s acceptance of an insurance policy or certifiicate provided by the Employer does not change the responsibility of the Employer to provide the insurances stated in the Contract Data.’

Wording of Clause 87.3

‘The Contractor may insure a risk which this contract requires the Employer to insure if the Employer does not submit a required policy or certificate.The cost of this insurance to the Contractor is paid by the Employer.’

Commentary on Clauses 85.1 to 87.3

The intentions of the wording of these clauses appear straightforward.

The Contractor and Employer both have to produce policies and certificates of the insurances required by their respective contractual obligations, before the starting date and periodically thereafter as requested. The Contractor’s insurances are submitted to the Project Manager; the Employer’s insurances are submitted to the Contractor.

If the insurances comply with the contract they are acceptable. Each party must comply with the terms and conditions of their policies.

If either party’s contractually required policy or certificate is not produced when stated by the contract, the other party may insure the risks involved and charge the defaulting party for the costs involved.
