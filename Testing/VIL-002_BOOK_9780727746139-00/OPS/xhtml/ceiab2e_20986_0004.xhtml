4 Requirement for third party liability insurance

It has been described already how a Contractor and/or an Employer (and others) might face liability claims for death or injury to persons (employees and third parties) or damage to third party property. However, there is little point in making claims against persons who lack the financial resources to deal with them. Therefore, to reduce the vulnerability of the Employer to claims which should fairly rest wholly or partly with a Contractor (who may not have the financial resources to support any indemnity given), there are requirements in ICE Conditions of Contract for a Contractor to take out insurance to cover liabilities. Those requirements are described in the following paragraphs.

ICE 6,Clause 23

Wording of Clause 23(1) — Third party insurance

‘The Contractor shall without limiting his or the Employer’s obligations and responsibilities under Clause 22 insure in the joint names of the Contractor and the Employer against liabilities for death of or injury to any person (other than any operative or other person in the employment of the Contractor or any of his sub-contractors) or loss of or damage to any property (other than the Works) arising out of the performance of the Contract other than those due to the exceptions defined in Clause 22(2)(a)(b)(c) and (d)’.

Commentary on Clause 23(1)

The insurance required under this Clause is for a Contractor’s third party public liability only. It excludes persons engaged by a Contractor and a Contractor’s sub-contractors, it excludes the Works, and it excludes exceptions (a) to (d) given in Clause 22(2), which are the responsibility of the Employer. (However, interestingly the Contractor’s third party liability insurance does not exclude (i.e. does include) the exception given in Clause 22(2)(e) — injury or damage due to an Employer’s neglect or breach of statutory duty. This appears to be a particularly onerous liability for a Contractor!) Although the policy must, according to Clause 23(1), be in the joint names of the Contractor and Employer, which thus enables the Employer to obtain third party cover under the Contractor’s policy, it is a case of coinsurance and not joint insurance. It also enables one party, such as the Employer, to make a claim on insurers even if the other party is unwilling to do so. However, public liability cover excludes injury to persons employed by the insured or damage to property of the insured, and therefore injury or damage caused by one insured party to the other insured would normally be excluded. This is overcome by ICE 6 also requiring a cross liability clause (see Clause 23(2), discussed below).

The implications of Clause 23(1) read in conjunction with Clause 22(1) are that insurance should be required to meet all losses and claims in respect of death or injury to any third party or damage to any third party property. However, a public liability policy does not cover all forms of damage to all property. The policy has limitations in extent of cover (e.g. it will have bottomend deductibles or excesses, and perhaps top-end ceilings per claim and annual limits), and exclusions (e.g. it will exclude injury or damage arising from radioactivity from any nuclear fuel (uninsurable in the commercial insurance market)), it will exclude injury arising from motor vehicles used on the road (for which compulsory motor insurance is required) and it will exclude liability arising out of the use of aircraft, ships or other vessels.

Seldom do the policies issued by insurers correspond exactly with the requirements of conditions of contract. A detailed analysis is therefore necessary by both Contractor and Employer to see what the deficiencies are in the Public Liability policy and whether those deficiencies are insured under other policies.

An Employer needs to be satisfied that a Contractor’s indemnity can be adequately financed if called upon. A Contractor also needs to be satisfied that indemnity obligations can be met, as liability remains whether or not insurance is in place. Complexities can arise because cover can be provided by a pre-agreed annual renewable Contractor’s policy, by a project policy, by a Contractor’s own captive insurance company, by a group of ‘lead’ and ‘following’ insurers or reinsurers, by a complicated risk financing programme including insured and self-insured portions, and so on. In many situations parties may need to obtain specialist advice to ensure that adequate indemnity provisions are actually being supplied.

Wording of Clause 23(2) — Cross liability

‘The insurance policy shall include a cross liability clause such that the insurance shall apply to the Contractor and to the Employer as separate insured’.

Commentary on Clause 23(2)

A cross liability clause enables the third party policy to be treated as if a separate policy has been issued to each of the parties, i.e. as if each party is a third party as far as the other is concerned. For example, if a joint-named Contractor damages a joint-named Employer’s property (other than the Works), because of the cross liability clause indemnity can be claimed by the Employer from the Contractor under the joint names policy.

Wording of Clause 23(3) — Amount of insurance

‘Such insurance shall be for at least the amount stated in the Appendix to the Form of Tender.’

Commentary on Clause 23(3)

The limit stated in the Appendix will be a minimum selected by an Employer, probably with the advice of others such as professional advisors. However, a Contractor will have unlimited liability in law and must determine what level of cover is required from the Public Liability insurance. The Contractor should undertake a risk assessment of possible hazardous events which might impact on third parties — to find out the financial implications, the possibilities of occurrence, what non-insured portion (e.g. deductible) needs to be covered by means other than insurance, the implications and exposures of other works being undertaken by the Contractor, and so on. In addition to these aspects, liability may be decided some years after a contract has been completed and the effects of inflation need to be taken into account. It must also be borne in mind that the extent of exposure bears no relationship to the contract value, with even small contracts producing large claims.

ICE Design and Construct,Clause 23

The wording is identical to Clause 23 of ICE 6.

ICE Minor Works,Clause 10.6

Wording of Clause 10.6 — Employer to approve insurance

‘The Contractor shall throughout the execution of the Works maintain insurance against damage loss or injury for which he is liable under Clause 10.2 subject to the exceptions provided by Clauses 10.3 and 10.4. ⋯ The terms of such insurance shall include a provision whereby in the event of any claim in respect of which the Contractor would be entitled to receive indemnity under the policy being brought or made against the Employer the insurer will indemnify the Employer against any such claims and any costs charges and expenses in respect thereof.

Commentary on Clause 10.6

It will be noted that unlike ICE 6, third party insurance is not in the joint names of the Contractor and Employer, nor is there a cross liability clause. Because the Employer is not, strictly speaking, a party to the policy, where a third party sues an Employer for an issue where the Contractor would be liable, the Employer cannot sue the insurer directly for indemnity. In practice an insurer will indemnify the Employer where the Contractor is liable and hence entitled to indemnity. If, however, the Employer was liable to a third party in circumstances where the Contractor was not liable, the Employer would be uninsured.

The Guidance Notes to ICE Minor Works advise a minimum limit of indemnity of £2 million for any one accident/unlimited number of accidents and an even higher limit at locations of greater risks. This is because, although the value of the Works may be small, the risk to third parties may be the same as for higher-value contracts.

NEC,Clause 84 — Insurance cover

Clause 84 in part relates to insurance to cover a Contractor’s liability for personal injury and damage to third party property.

Wording of Clause 84.1

‘The Contractor provides the insurances stated in the Insurance Table except any insurance which the Employer is to provide as stated in the Contract Data. The Contractor provides additional insurances as stated in the Contract Data.’

Insurance Table

Insurance against Minimum amount of cover or minimum limit of indemnity
Liability for loss of or damage to property (except the works, Plant and Materials and Equipment) and liability for bodily injury to or death of a person (not an employee of the Contractor) caused by activity in connection with this contract. The amount stated in the Contract Data for any one event with cross liability so that the insurance applies to the Parties separately.

Wording of Clause 84.2

‘The insurances are in the joint names of the Parties and provide cover for events which are due to the Contractor’s risk from the starting date until the Defects Certificate has been issued.’

Contract Data Part one — Data provided by the Employer

Paragraph 8 in part relates to Employer requirements for the Contractor’s insurance cover of liabilities to third parties. The relevant part-wording is:

‘The amount of the minimum limit of indemnity for insurance in respect of loss of or damage to property (except the works, Plant and Materials and Equipment) and liability for bodily injury to or death of a person (not an employee of the Contractor) due to activity in connection with this contract for any one event is ..........’.

One of the ‘Optional Statements’ enables the Employer, instead of the Contractor, to provide insurance against third party liabilities. It requires the Employer to fill in details of the indemnity to be provided and any applicable deductibles.

Commentary on Clauses 84.1 and 84.2 and Paragraph 8 of the Contract Data

The Contractor must provide insurance against liability for third party injury or death to persons or loss of or damage to property, unless the Employer has stated in the Contract Data that the Employer will provide that insurance. The insurance covers persons who are employees of the Employer, and property (other than the works) which belongs to the Employer.

The Employer states the minimum amount of cover required per event. Paragraph 8 appears to require one amount, whether the event applies to persons or property. A site in a remote overseas location might require a different property indemnity from one in the centre of a Western city. In addition, the value of property indemnity might be totally different from the indemnity required for liability for injury or death to persons. The Employer should therefore consider for his project whether giving two indemnity values in Paragraph 8 is more appropriate, i.e. one for persons and one for property.

Where the Contractor is providing third party property insurance, no limit is placed on the deductible permitted; however, where the Employer is providing cover instead, a deductible is stated.

The insurance is a joint names policy with cross liability. (It will be apparent from Chapter 1 that the insureds are, in insurance terms, co-insureds, not joint-insureds.) As described earlier, the cross liability permits the cover to apply to the Employer’s employees or property (excluding the works) where injury or damage is caused by the Contractor, even though the Employer is named as an insured in the policy.
