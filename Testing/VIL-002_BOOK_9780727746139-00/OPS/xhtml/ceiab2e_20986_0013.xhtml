13 Performance bonds

Performance bonds

A party to a construction contract can partially protect itself from the consequences of premature termination of that contract (perhaps because of solvency or severe financial difficulties) or some other failure to perform contractual obligations by the other party to the contract, by requiring the latter to provide a performance bond. Occasionally, and more often overseas, a Contractor can require a bond from an Employer; it is also possible for a strongly positioned specialist Sub-Contractor to require a bond from a main Contractor. However, in construction contracts, by far the most usual performance bond is one required by an Employer of a main Contractor. The bond is commonly for 10% of a contract value, but the reason for this percentage (compared with any other) is unclear.

Many construction conditions of contract, including ICE 6 and ICE Design and Construct, incorporate suggested Forms of Bond. An Employer has an option, often exercised, to require a Contractor to provide such a bond. The third party bond provider, usually a banker or insurance company acting as what is known as a Surety, will either provide a bond using the standard contract form of words or, if permitted by the Employer, may provide a bond using his own terminology. The Surety binds himself with the Contractor for a stated period of time to pay losses up to an agreed amount sustained by an Employer if the Contractor does not perform his obligations under the contract.

A bond therefore involves three parties, a Surety, a Contractor and an Employer. A bond should be drafted so that it remains in force until the obligations of the Contractor have been completed. In fact most bonds stipulate a date upon which they cease, usually the date of the anticipated end of a maintenance period.

Categories of bonds

There are two basic categories of performance bond. There are ‘on-demand’ bonds, which – at their simplest without conditions attached – may be called upon by an Employer even when sometimes there may not be a reasonable cause. For instance, a ‘falling out’ between an Employer and a Contractor, particularly on overseas contracts, might lead to an unjustifiable call on an on-demand bond. In other words, an on-demand bond, if it is unconditional, can be cashed in at any time the Employer wishes, without the Contractor being in default of his/her obligations under the contract. A Contractor has to pay high premiums for such bonds. On-demands bonds are clearly very onerous and are usually only agreed to with reluctance if required for strong commercial reasons, e.g. if agreement to have such bonds is essential in order to be awarded a contract.

The other category of performance bond is an ‘on-default’ bond, which can be called upon when a Contractor demonstrably fails to undertake obligations under a contract, i.e. the Contractor ‘defaults’.

Both bonds are thus undertakings given by a Surety to make payment to an Employer (or otherwise ensure performance) where a Contractor fails to meet an Employer’s expectations or contractual requirements. They are therefore forms of guarantee which are evidenced in writing. Although a Contractor pays the bond premium, the Employer is ultimately paying for it as it will be reflected in the Contractor’s price for the contract. Employers should bear this in mind when deciding on which type (‘on-demand’ or ‘on-default’) they require.

It should be noted that a Contractor cannot escape the financial consequences of default by providing a bond, since a Surety has common law rights of recovery against the Contractor. These are usually reinforced by the Surety requiring a written form of counter-indemnity or other security from the Contractor, or the Contractor’s parent company.

Duty of Employer to disclose information

A Surety may be released from his obligations if there is a dishonest failure by an Employer to disclose material information which would affect the Surety’s decision of whether or not to provide a bond. Since bonds are not contracts of insurance, there is no general duty on the Employer to disclose all material information as would be the case under an insurance policy. However, a bond may be invalid if it is obtained by Employer fraud or dishonesty concealing material facts relating to risks known to the Employer but which the Surety would not reasonably be expected to know existed. An example would be that the Employer knew that the tender price was far too low in comparison with other tenders because of some mistake by the Contractor.

Sub-Contractors

A main Contractor is responsible for completing the Works and even if the Contractor is prevented from doing so because of poor performance by a Sub-Contractor, a bond will remain valid. A main Contractor may therefore consider demanding bonds from significant Sub-Contractors.

Issuers of bonds

Bonds are usually issued by banks or insurance companies. There are advantages and disadvantages attaching to both.

Banks regard bonds as an extension of their line of credit to a Contractor. They usually know a Contractor better than an insurance company and can therefore issue bonds speedily. However, when issued by a bank, the bond may affect the availability of that bank’s credit to that Contractor, perhaps prejudicing the advance of money in the future to purchase plant and materials, or on other projects taking place during the same risk period. Banks usually pay out on bonds more promptly, as they are less willing than an insurance company to argue or investigate the merits of any claim.

An insurance company will usually issue a bond only after it has made careful enquiries into a Contractor’s financial stability and ability to perform a Contract. The performance of these enquiries is reassuring to an Employer since it indicates that if an insurance company has issued a bond it is satisfied with the risk. Conversely if an insurance company refuses to issue a bond and an Employer is aware of that situation, the Employer might question whether the right Contractor has been selected.

A bond is typically for 10% of the Contract Price, higher or lower amounts may be requested, or no bond may be required at all. An Employer has to decide what percentage is sufficient.

Other forms of bond

There are several other forms of bond.

  • A bid (or tender) bond is intended to reassure an Employer that a bid is a responsible one and that if the bid is accepted the tenderer will proceed to execute a contract. If the tenderer fails to do so, and an Employer incurs loss and expense in the retendering process, those losses could form a claim under the bond.
  • Advanced-payment, progress or repayment bonds protect an Employer against any advanced monies being lost through default by the Contractor.
  • Retention bonds are bonds issued in lieu of retention monies. They can be used to permit the early release of the retention element of monthly progress payments.
  • Maintenance bonds protect an Employer as an alternative to retention monies during a maintenance period. They provide reassurance that a Contractor will carry out residual obligations during the maintenance period of a Contract.

ICE 6 – Form of Bond

The Form of Bond given in ICE 6 is shown in Appendix 4. The Contractor and Surety are ‘held and firmly bound’ to the Employer. This is joint and several liability and the Employer may sue one or both of the parties for the full amount of the loss.

The bond recognises that the Contractor has agreed with the Employer to enter into a Contract for the construction, completion and maintenance of the Works. If the Contractor duly performs and observes the terms of the Contract the bond is null and void. If the Contractor defaults on the Contract and the Surety satisfies the Employer’s loss, the bond is again null and void. But if the Contractor continues not to fulfil contractual obligations, the balance of the bond will remain available to the Employer until the Contractor does fulfil them.

The bond also provides that ‘no alteration in the terms of the said Contract made by agreement between the Employer and the Contractor or in the extent or nature of the Works to be constructed and completed thereunder and no allowance of time by the Employer or the Engineer under the said Contract nor any forbearance or forgiveness in or in respect of any matter or thing concerning the said Contract . . . shall in any way release the Surety from any liability under the . . . Bond.’ Without this requirement the common law position, i.e. that changes in the risk covered release the Surety, would apply.

As the ICE 6 bond is not meant to be an ‘on-demand’ bond, an Employer has to prove a loss before a Surety pays.

ICE Design and Construct

The Form of Bond is set out in slightly clearer terms in part compared with the bond in the ICE 6. However, there is no significant difference in effect between the two versions.

ICE Minor Works

There is no Form of Bond included within ICE Minor Works. It is presumably considered that other existing provisions, such as payment in arrears for work done and retentions, provide sufficient security. (Indeed, there is a view that perhaps there is sufficient practical Employer risk protection within most types of contract anyway, without the need for additional premium to be paid to Sureties. Employers could increase retentions if not wholly convinced. The provision of bonds certainly appears to be a practice that needs review by Employers to determine what protection is really necessary, considering that it is the Employer who ultimately pays the additional premium bill.)

NEC

There is provision for a performance bond in Clause Gl.l of NEC. Clause Gl.l is given in ‘Secondary Option’ Clause G.

Wording of Clause Gl.l

‘TheContractor gives the Employer a performance bond, provided by a bank or insurer which the Project Manager has accepted, for the amount stated in the Contract Data and in the form set out in the Works Information. A reason for not accepting the bank or insurer is that its commercial position is not strong enough to carry the bond. If the bond was not given by the Contract Date, it is given to the Employer within four weeks of the Contract Date.’

Within the Contract Data, Part one, ‘Data provided by the Employer’ is an Optional Statement, headed ‘If Option G is used’. The Employer must state the value of the performance bond required.

Commentary on Secondary Option Clause G

The provision of a performance bond is optional. If an Employer decides to have one it must be in the form set out in the Works Information (defined in Clause 11.2(5) of NEC), and to the amount defined in the Option Statement. This Clause leaves the decision to require an ICE 6 type of bond, or any other, open to the Employer.

The bond is to be provided to the Employer within four weeks of the Contract Date, otherwise the Contractor is in breach of contract.

A potential problem arises in practice as to how to assess and then to refuse to accept a Surety on the basis that ‘its commercial position is not strong enough’. The purpose of this sentence is clear, and it could be particularly relevant to overseas suretyproviding organisations whose credentials may be unknown. However, should a Project Manager refuse to accept a Surety which turns out to be sound, this would appear to leave the Project Manager open to legal proceedings. If the Project Manager refuses for other reasons, this issue would become an NEC ‘compensation event’ under Clause 60.1(a).

Developments, 1993 to 1995

For many years most standard construction Forms of Bond, including those of the ICE, were worded in a similar manner. All parties had a perceived understanding of what was intended by those words and that they were dealing with ‘on-default’ bonds. However, in 1993 and 1994 several legal cases arose where decisions and commentaries were both critical of standard wording and threw into doubt previous perceived understanding, particularly of what constituted sufficient ‘default’ to enable bonds to be drawn upon.

It was finally left to the House of Lords in 1995 to confirm that Forms of Bond such as those of the ICE were, in effect, default bonds which required a proven breach of contract and incurred damages, against which Sureties could set-off valid counterclaim costs. One problem with this is that there can be considerable delays before a Surety needs to pay out, and that negates one of the objectives of a bond, i.e. to provide an injured party with the finances to proceed as soon as possible.

The courts in 1994 were strongly critical of the confusing and ‘archaic’ forms of words used in typical construction bonds. As a result Sureties, such as those who are members of the Association of British Insurers (ABI), discussed the use of different and specific forms of wording. The intention was that the wording would clarify that, where intended, bonds will definitely be understood to be ‘on-default’ not ‘on-demand’, and that net losses must be proved after taking into account other monies owed under a contract. In parallel the Conditions of Contract Standing Joint Committee (CCSJC) of the ICE, the Association of Consulting Engineers and the Federation of Civil Engineering Contractors also considered the wording for a new ICE Form of Bond which was intended to be comprehensive, to enable prompt payment to be made where there is a clear entitlement, to state the expiratory date and to reflect definitions of terms used in corresponding main contracts so as to avoid misinterpretation and confusion. At the time of writing, a new ICE Form of Bond has yet to be published.
