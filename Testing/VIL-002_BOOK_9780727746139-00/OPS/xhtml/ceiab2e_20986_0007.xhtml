7 A Contractor's Employer's Liability policy

A specimen Employer's Liability policy is included in Appendix 2. It is numbered in sections to make the following text easier to follow. As with all other insurance policies, there is no standard policy and they vary from insurer to insurer. The policy reproduced in Appendix 2 is, however, typical of those in daily use.

An Employer's Liability policy protects a contractor, as an employer, against legal liability for death of, injury to or disease sustained by the contractor's employees in the course of their employment.

Employer's Liability insurance in the United Kingdom is compulsory by virtue of the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969. There are certain exceptions, such as employers who are government bodies. The intention behind compulsory insurance is that if an injured employee has a valid claim for damages against an employer, compulsory insurance will see that the employee obtains compensation regardless of the financial status of the employer.

Unlimited cover

There are certain technical regulations laid down in the Act, but they are not relevant to this chapter. Although the Act stipulates that the minimum limit of indemnity under compulsory Employers' Liability insurance shall be £2 million for any one occurrence, until 1995 insurers continued, in practice, to provide indemnity unlimited in amount. From 1995 the latter position changed and a limit of £10 million per occurrence was imposed by insurers. A higher limit of indemnity is available from some insurers, up to possibly £25 million, depending on the nature of the risk.

The Act provides that a Certificate of Insurance must be issued to prove that the employer is insured, and copies of that Certificate must be displayed at the insured's place of business or, where he has more than one place of business, at each place of business where he employs people.

Who is an employee?

In modern times it has become increasingly difficult to recognise who is an employee so as to create a legal employer/employee relationship.

Doubts can arise in deciding whether a person is engaged under a contract of service so as to be an employee, or under a contract for services so as to be an independent contractor.

Over the years the courts have laid down many tests for deciding whether a person is an employee. It is, therefore, relevant to see who engages the employee, pays wages, determines entitlement to holidays or pays National Insurance contributions, regulates work and has the power of dismissal. In the main, the deciding factor has been the ‘control' test. An employee is subject to the control of an employer as to the manner in which the work is done, since the employer can tell the employee what to do and how to do it. However, in the case of highly skilled employees, the control test is hardly appropriate. It cannot be said, for example, that a skilled surgeon is subject to the control of an employer over the way the surgeon's medical work is carried out. Nor can it be said that other highly skilled people are under the control of their employer. Thus on occasions the ‘integration test' has been used. Was the work done by the employed person integral to the employer's business or merely accessory to it? If it was integral there would be an employer/ employee relationship but this is not necessarily so if it was merely accessory to it. But that test, too, is not perfect. In the construction industry (where there is much use of labour gangs, labour-only contractors and other self-employed individuals), borderline cases often arise. No matter what label is given or used by a person, e.g. ‘self-employed contractor', the courts will look behind the label to ascertain the true relationship.

Section 1 – The opening clause

This is similar to the clause seen in connection with the Public Liability Policy. Again it makes the proposal for insurance the basis of the contract, so that any inaccuracy may invalidate the policy. Again it is important to note that it is only ‘the business' described in the schedule which is covered. A firm described as a building contractor will not necessarily be covered when operating as a civil engineering contractor.

Section 2 – Employees

  • The cover relates only to employees under a contract of service or apprenticeship with the contractor, i.e. where there is an employer/employee relationship.

    • As already mentioned, labour gangs and self-employed contractors often fall into a grey area and many policies are endorsed to the effect that such persons and those mentioned in Exclusion 1 to Section 4 of the Public Liability policy shall be deemed to be employees under a contract of service or apprenticeship for the purposes of the policy. Thus the insured's liability for accident or injury to these workers is covered.

  • The policy relates only to employees who are employed in Great Britain, Northern Ireland, the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands, or temporarily working abroad. Doubts can arise over the word ‘temporary'. In the case of long-term contracts abroad it is necessary to clarify the position with the insurers. Often local legislation may dictate that policies have to be arranged with insurers in the country where the contract is being performed.
  • The employer is protected only against claims for bodily injury or disease. Property damage is not covered, although in practice, where an employee sustains damage to his clothing and personal effects at the same time as he is injured, such damage is usually paid in addition to compensation for the injuries.
  • The bodily injury or disease has to be caused during the period of insurance. The word ‘caused' is used deliberately with diseases in mind. Injury consequent upon an accident is usually immediately apparent. There are some diseases, however, such as asbestosis, cancer and noise-induced hearing loss, which may be caused over a long period of time and may not be diagnosed until 20 or 30 years later. Provided the disease was caused during the period of insurance, the policy is operative. An employer must, therefore, be able to trace policies and the insurers for many years back (in some cases 30 years) to ascertain which insurance cover was in place when the disease was caused. The policies, if there are more than one, then share the claim, depending upon how much of the disease was ‘caused' during the periods for which each of the policies was in force – a difficult point to establish. It is sometimes possible to extend a policy to cover claims made during the period of validity of a policy for diseases caused before its inception, but not all insurers are prepared to give this cover. If earlier causation or cover cannot be determined, or no ‘claims made' cover agreed, then a contractor will be liable, without insurers'; support, for the damages attributable to the uninsured period.
  • The injury or disease must arise out of and in the course of employment with the contractor. Where an employee is injured in out-of-work activities and has a valid claim against an employer, that employer will usually find insurance protection within the Public Liability policy, and not under the Employer's Liability policy.
  • The bodily injury or disease to be covered must arise in connection with the business described in the policy – hence the importance of having the business correctly described.

Section 3 – The indemnity

Paragraph 1

This states that the insurers will indemnify an employer against liability at law in respect of injury or disease to employees. The expression ‘liability at law' covers all forms of liability, i.e. the policy is not restricted to negligence. The policy is, however, a strictly legal liability policy. It is not a policy which simply makes up the employee's loss of wages while away from work because the employer feels there is a moral obligation to do so.

A stipulation that for employees working abroad a claim must be brought in the courts of the United Kingdom is not always included in policies; if possible, it should be removed since it limits the cover.

Paragraph 2

The policy protects an insured against additional liability imposed by construction contracts with Principals (such as the Employer) and where necessary indemnifies the Principal in the same way as the insured. Thus a Principal (Employer) becomes in effect an insured person under a Contractor's policy. It is necessary to add here that under ICE 6 there is no requirement for a Contractor to insure, or for an Employer to be a named insured under a Contractor's Employer's Liability policy.

Section 4 – Legal costs

In addition to paying the damages resulting from a valid claim by an injured employee, the insurers will also pay the legal costs and expenses incurred in handling and negotiating settlement of the claim.

The final paragraph of Section 4 deals with a technicality which arises from the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act, which was passed to make sure that an injured employee obtained compensation. Prior to the Act, there were circumstances where insurers could refuse to pay under their policy because the insured was in breach of the policy conditions, e.g. by not notifying a claim to the insurers in time. In such circumstances the injured person still had a common law claim for damages against the employer but the employer might not be in a financial position to pay them. The employee, therefore, would not receive compensation.

The Act, therefore, limited the right of an insurer to repudiate liability in certain circumstances. For example, where an insured fails to comply with the conditions requiring prompt notification of a claim to insurers, or failed to take reasonable care to prevent injury to employees, the insurer must pay the injured employee and recover the payment from the insured.

As explained in Chapter 5, all policies have conditions which must be complied with. A breach of these conditions may give the insurer the legal right to avoid the claim.

Other points

  • The person indemnified by the policy is the Employer, normally a limited liability company. Most policies, however, indemnify directors, managers and employees as well. Such persons would be at risk for two reasons. First, they have a duty to their employer under their contracts of service to perform their work reasonably and competently so as not to cause loss to the employer. Thus, if by the negligence of one employee a fellow employee is injured, resulting in the employer paying damages, the employer having paid those damages can legally recover them from the negligent employee. If the insurer pays, then under the principle of subrogation the insurer can sue the employee for repayment. In practice there are agreements to the effect that an insurer will not do so.

    • Secondly, each person, even though an employee, is responsible for his or her own act of negligence. Thus, if one employee negligently injures a fellow employee, there is nothing to stop the negligent employee being sued personally even though the employer would be vicariously liable as well.

    By extending the policy to grant an indemnity to directors, managers and employees, in addition to the named insured, such other persons are protected.

  • Directors who are employed under a contract of service with the insured are regarded as employees in the same way as any other employee. In the case of outside directors who may not be under a contract of service, the policy can be altered to treat these people as though they were directors under a contract of service.
  • Exclusions in the policy may apply either because a contractor states that a type of work is not undertaken or because the contractor is not prepared to pay the premium to include that type of work. Thus the policy may exclude, for example, an indemnity for employer's liability to employees engaged on demolition, tunnelling or work at certain heights or certain depths in the ground.
