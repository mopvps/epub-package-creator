16 Risk management and insurance

Commercial basis of risk management

A ‘hazard’ is something that can occur with a resulting adverse consequence. A ‘risk’ is the multiple of that hazardous consequence and its possibility of occurrence. In other words, a hazard of likely maximum adverse consequence of £1 000 000 but with a 1 in 10 probability of occurrence is a £100 000 risk. Different risks can thus be placed in ascending order of importance and decisions taken on if and how to manage them.

In a commercial world, risk management costs must be cost-effective. For example, it makes little sense to spend £300 000 on risk reduction methods such as fire protection if the value of the risk is only £100 000. Risk management costs must also compete for funds with other demands on a company’s finance; an investment of £50 000 to eliminate a possible risk of £100 000 will not be competitive with an investment of £50 000 to make increased profits of £200 000.

The management of risk by insurance must be shown to be the most cost-effective of available commercial solutions, other than where insurance is a statutory requirement and cannot be avoided.

Hazards

Hazards can arise from outside an organisation (e.g. poor quality of service by outside suppliers or sub-contractors), can exist inside it (e.g. accidents to employees, physical damage to plant) or can be transmitted from the organisation to those outside (e.g. injury or damage by construction works to third parties).

Hazards can include the following categories.

  • Physical damage: damage or loss to property caused by fire, theft and weather.
  • Legal liabilities: breach of common law (e.g. negligence), of statute (e.g. health and safety), of contract conditions. Liabilities exist to employees, third parties and the State for breach of legal requirements, whether or not injury or damage results.
  • Consequential loss: loss of profits following direct losses such as physical damage.
  • Financial loss: inadequate forecasts, estimates, marketing or investment decisions, creditor or debtor management and provisions to cover hazards occurring.
  • Sociopolitical developments: Government policy changes including subsidies and taxation, more public awareness of environmental issues, protest groups and public enquiry delays.
  • Labour problems: strikes, working to rule.
  • Transport hazards: motor, marine and air delays or losses to and from work premises/site.
  • Internal management problems: poor recruitment, training, control, quality and delivery of products.
  • Contracts with others: risks accepted in contracts which are not transferred to others.
  • Overseas difficulties: unfamiliar territory, transportation problems, exchange rates and control variations, political instability and legal differences.
  • Construction hazards

Each industry will have its own problems. Construction will have general problems associated with all types of building work (e.g. quality of work, management of sub-contractors), problems associated with the various types of construction (e.g. roadworks, bridges, marine works, power stations) and problems associated with the particular site on which work is being carried out.

Management of hazards

The basic management procedures are simple to list:

  • Identify hazards.

  • Determine risks in order of priority.
  • Eliminate, reduce or transfer to others as many significant risks as is economically possible.

Insurance is only one possible part of a proactive organisation risk management programme. It is commonly used in conjunction with several of the other forms of financing of residual risks once as many risks as are commercially viable have been eliminated, reduced or contractually transferred to others. Insurance, however, will inevitably be a significant part of construction risk management because of statutory and contractual insurance requirements. In addition it will often prove to be the most cost-effective or most convenient method to manage certain classes of risk, or risks above a certain level.

Insurers can make use of a specialist reinsurance market to offset risks, and some larger construction organisations and clients have captives (insurance subsidiaries of non-insurance parent companies) which deal directly with that reinsurance market. Such matters are really outside the scope of the present text but readers should be aware that such organisations exist, usually in the background to construction organisations’ direct insurance activities.

As insurance is the topic covered by this book, its role in current and future risk management will now be considered in slightly more detail.

Nature of insurance

Traditionally insurance cover is provided by the insured parties who, with similar risks, have paid premiums in proportion to those risks into a common pool managed by insurers. The insurers determine the premium they need on the basis of what money would be needed to be paid out on valid claims, adding additional percentages to the premium to cover management overheads and profit margins. In other words, taking the pool as a whole, in the long run what is paid in by the insureds is paid out to insureds, with insurers taking a certain percentage en route. Insurance is therefore a cost-smoothing mechanism, insurers exchanging an unknown fluctuating annual loss for a regular known annual premium. In bad years individual insureds, via insurers, are therefore supported by the luckier insureds or by funds built up in good years. Insurance has always been a ‘sleep soundly’ option and few have been faulted for buying insurance to cover organisational risks, as long as the premium was reasonably competitive compared with other insurers for the same amount of cover.

However, relatively recently in the UK, commercial organisations have started to investigate other options to insurance. This has been brought about by falling profit margins making all organisational expenditure subject to more detailed scrutiny, by insurance premiums becoming more expensive, by insurance contract conditions becoming more onerous (e.g. larger excesses), and by the extent of cover being limited by insurers or not even being readily available for some classes of risk.

Some of the main disadvantages perceived of insurance include:

  • Riskier areas, particularly those with long-tail liabilities, such as pollution, are difficult if not impossible to insure.
  • Generally a ‘pool’ system does not reward a consistently good claims record and thus there is no incentive for an organisation with such a record to undertake effective risk management.
  • Insurance can be costly with a high mark-up, typically 30% to 40%, included within premium.
  • There are bottom excesses (or deductibles) and top ceilings beyond which self-financing is still required by the insured.
  • Insurers are often perceived to offer a poor claims settling service.
  • Insurance cannot cover criminal liability and resulting fines and prison sentences.
  • Generally insurance is renegotiable annually, with the possibility of unexpectedly more onerous terms and changes in cover being imposed.

Trends in the use of construction insurances as a method of risk management

Buyers of insurances are becoming increasingly aware of the foregoing disadvantages of trying to rely almost solely on insurance to manage company risks. The better organisations no longer want to belong to a pool where they subsidise more poorly managed ones. They see little point in paying annual premiums for regular and/or readily estimated losses (e.g. internal car fleet losses) to see those premiums returned to them after up to one-third of the premium has been deducted for insurer’s overheads and profits — this is known as ‘pound swapping’. They prefer instead to keep the premium in their own accounts, earning interest until claims are actually made. Organisations are therefore tending to use other more financially advantageous forms of risk financing (which may include subsidiary captive insurance companies), reserving regular insurance for the more catastrophic, unpredictable losses.

Insurers, on the other hand, are being left on balance with the less risk-conscious organisations and larger, more volatile risks. They are having to match the requirement for continuing market share of premiums (needed to justify their existence) with a need to charge adequate rates to achieve profit (needed to justify continued trading). The present trend is towards increased premiums and more onerous restrictions on cover, not least by requiring larger excesses/deductibles and lower ceilings on cover. This trend is reflected in the construction market.

Construction organisations, particularly contractors, have limited room for manoeuvre outside insurance. They need to comply with both statutory and contractual insurance cover, even if the cover is becoming more difficult, if not impossible, to obtain at what were historically accepted in the industry as reasonable costs of premium.

To maintain market share, insurers are increasingly taking into account the claims track record of individual construction-related organisations, in order to provide the best possible deal. To reduce insured claims, insurers are also slowly tending towards taking more interest in how contractors propose tackling more hazardous areas of contracts, and how they are actually doing so. This is evidenced in building works, now occasionally sold with Latent Defects insurance and where independent assessors are employed to monitor risky areas of construction on behalf of insurers. A report in 1994 regarded this as an essential development, to the extent of proposing having Building Latent Defect insurance made a compulsory contract requirement. Whether this would be successful (e.g. no-one can instruct insurers to provide insurance where they do not want to — say because a contractor has little track record in a particular area, because of a poor contractor claims record or because a project is too large or a ‘one-ofT of indeterminate risk) remains to be seen.

The trend in construction risk management involving insurance must inevitably be towards better control of insurable hazards by insureds, and more risk control and monitoring by insurers. With more restrictions on cover, employers will need to scrutinise more fully what is actually being supplied by others to meet contractual insurance requirements, and how uninsured portions will be financed. In the long term, particularly for large projects, this can mean more specific project insurances covering all parties with comprehensive non-duplicating, gap-free cover. Perhaps in the more distant future, larger cash-rich employers will increasingly provide that project cover from within their own internal risk management programmes.
