Insurance

Contractors’ All Risks insurance

The insurance of civil engineering works and of liabilities arising during and in consequence of the carrying out of the works is usually by a Contractors’ All Risks (‘CAR’) policy. Such a policy will be a combination of the first, second and, sometimes, the third of the following elements:

  • Material Damage insurance, which is insurance of the works which are being constructed, including materials for incorporation into the works. It is also called ‘Contract Works’ insurance.
    • Public Liability insurance, which is insurance of the liability of the Contractor for personal injury, damage to property and other torts.
    • Employer’s Liability insurance, which is insurance of the Contractor’s liability for injury to the Contractor’s employees.

    Public and Employer’s Liability insurances are called ‘liability policies’ because they cover the insured’s liability for personal injury and damage to property, not the persons or property in question.

    Where a project of significant size is involved there will usually be a project policy which will insure the Employer, Contractor and Sub-Contractors involved in the project. A project policy will usually be for the duration of the project and may extend cover into any maintenance period. Contractors normally, however, have an annual policy (alternatively called a floater policy) which provides insurance cover under one policy for most of their usual or smaller projects. The general provisions found in both project and annual CAR policies are similar. Project policies may, however, contain special provisions tailored for a particular project. Contract Works, Public Liability and Employer’s Liability policies will each be discussed in more detail later in this text.

    The insurance market

    The United Kingdom insurance market consists of insurance companies and of underwriting syndicates at Lloyd’s, who underwrite the risks; insurance brokers acting for the insured who place business with the insurers; and loss adjusters appointed on behalf of the insurers to investigate and negotiate the settlement of claims.

    There are hundreds of insurers both in the United Kingdom and outside, in addition to many Lloyd’s underwriters, competing for insurance business. They do not compete only on price. The scope of the cover they offer, the terms and conditions to which it is subject, the service they are prepared to give (particularly in the handling of claims) and their reputation (for instance for fair and prompt settlement of claims) also come into play.

    Construction insurers

    While many insurers have a limited knowledge of the civil engineering and construction industry, there are some who specialise in construction and engineering insurance. For example, the insurers in the London Market willing to underwrite civil engineering and construction insurance have a significant degree of expertise in the insuring of civil engineering and construction risks. In some cases the underwriters (the people who consider the risk proposals and negotiate and agree the terms of the policy) are engineers or have engineering expertise available in-house. In other cases they will have a working knowledge of how a construction project is undertaken. It is now common for a CAR policy for a specific project or for contractor’s larger annual policies to be underwritten by several co-insurers from the CAR market.

    Insurance brokers

    The insurance broker is the agent for the insured and acts as the insured’s professional adviser in the nature and placing of the insurance required, whether it is for the project or for the contractor. As with insurers, one finds brokers who specialise in civil engineering insurance. There expertise enables them not only to indentify insurers most likely to accept the risk but to negotiate particular terms of the insurance contract to suit a client’s needs. Some of the larger contractors have subsidiary companies who act as brokers and deal with insurance matters relating to their contractor parent company.

    Loss adjusters

    Loss adjusters are appointed by insurers to investigate, negotiate and settle claims under the terms of the insurance policy. The larger firms have engineers and surveyors on their staff. Loss adjusters should not be confused with loss assessors who are engaged by, and act solely for, an insured in the preparation, presentation and negotiation of claims in order to obtain the best settlement for their client.

    Extent of financial risk

    In many civil engineering contracts the value of the contract works at risk from loss or damage is high. Moreover, claims for third party common law damages from people who have sustained injury or damage to their property as a result of construction activities are on the increase. Such risks are invariably covered by insurance. Payment of very large amounts of money by insurers may often depend upont he interpretation given to a word or phrase in a policy, or the information given by the insured when proposing the insurance or completing a proposal form.

    Unfortunately many organisations have a casual approach to insurance. It has a low order of priority. As a result insufficient thought is often given to the matter by the insured, so that the information given to the insurer to underwrite the risk, or the replies to questions asked by the insurer, are incomplete or inadequate. This can have a disastrous effect on the validity of the policies and the payment of claims, as will be explained later.

    Some basic insurance principles

    Insurable interest

    A basic principle of insurance law is that before a person can effect an insurance policy, that person must have an insurable interest in the subject matter. This means that the person must suffer some detriment if the subject matter is damaged, or must benefit from its continued well-being. Thus the owner of property is prejudiced if it is lost or stolen.

    However, person A cannot insure person B’s property against damage, since in the event of its being lost or stolen, person A has no interest in it. If person A were able to insure person B’s property, person A would clearly benefit by its destruction. The payment of insurance monies to person A would be contrary to public policy and such a policy would be illegal and void.

    Disclosure of material facts

    Like other forms of contract, insurance is subject to the normal contractual rules of offer and acceptance, consideration (the premium), legality, agreement of the parties, contractual capacity of the parties and the intention to create a legal relationship. Insurance contracts, however, differ fundamentally from other commercial contracts in the sense that they are bound by the principle of utmost good faith. In other words, there is a duty on the insured to disclose to the insurer all facts material to the risk which are known to the insured and not to the insurer.

    A material fact is one which would have had an effect (although not necessarily decisive) on the mind of a prudent underwriter, not necessarily the one who agreed the policy, in agreeing to accept the risk or not, or in deciding the amount of premium to charge. Whether or not the insured considered the fact to be material is not relevant. It is a test of the prudent underwriter, not the prudent insured. If there is a non-disclosure of a material fact and if the insurer can show that the underwriter was induced to agree to issue a policy on its relevant terms, the insurer may avoid the policy ab initio — from inception. In practice the requirement to show inducement is satisfied where the insurer can prove either that the policy would not have been issued or that the policy terms would have been different, had there been full and proper disclosure.

    The insured has a duty to disclose all material facts within his knowledge, whether actual or presumed, which have a bearing on the risk proposed and must make no misrepresentation about those facts or the risk. This includes all those facts which in the ordinary course of business affairs the insured knows or ought to have known about.

    Insurers often insist upon a proposal form being completed before insurance is offered. This asks a number of relevant questions which are material to the risk. The important point to note is that even though a question is not asked, if there is something material to the risk then the insured must disclose it. Moreover, most proposal forms contain a declaration and warranty at the foot of the form, to be signed by the insured, affirming that he has answered all questions accurately and not withheld material information. The effect of signing this declaration and warranty is that the insured warrants the accuracy of all the answers on the proposal form and further warrants that there has been no withholding of, or failure to disclose, material facts. A warranty in insurance law must be strictly complied with; in the event of a breach of warranty the policy, depending upon its terms, will either be void from the date of the breach or be voidable ad initio, at the discretion of the insurer, regardless of whether the breach gives rise to a loss or claim. Any inaccuracy in the proposal form is a breach of warranty which gives an insurer the right to treat the policy as void ab initio. Hence the importance of making sure that all answers to questions and information shown on proposal forms are correct.

    Over the years many technical and legal rules have been established in reaction to non-disclosure. Generally the following will be held to be material and must, therefore, be disclosed:

    • facts indicating that the subject matter of the insurance is exposed to more than the ordinary degree of risk, e.g. that the works are being constructed in an area particularly susceptible to flooding which might not otherwise be apparent from other information supplied;
    • facts indicating that the insured is activated by some special motive as, for example, where the value of property is greatly over-stated. In the absence of explanation, over-insurance may be indicative of an intent to create a loss with a view to a claim on the policy;
    • facts showing that the liability of the insurer is greater than would normally have been expected, e.g. that very valuable property is contained in a building with a poor standard of security and in an area where there has been a high incidence of theft;
    • facts showing that there is a moral hazard attaching to the insured suggesting that he is not a fit person to whom insurance can be granted, e.g. a person with a bad criminal history, particularly if it involves dishonesty, arson or reckless behaviour;
    • facts which to the insured’s knowledge are regarded by the insurers as material, such as high incidence of theft on a contract site.

    On the other hand there are facts which, although material, may become immaterial in certain circumstances and there is, therefore, no obligation upon the insured to disclose them. For example:

    • facts which are already known to the insurer or which the insurer may be reasonably presumed to know, e.g. that work at height or depth is hazardous;
    • facts which the insurer could have discovered by making some enquiries; for example, if the insurer is told that demolition work is to be carried out, the insurer has the opportunity to investigate the risks involved if required;
    • facts where the insurer has waived further information, where for example the insurer is told that a certain contractor is being used to perform the work and that contractor is known to be unsatisfactory;
    • facts tending to lessen the risk — for obvious reasons, since anything that lessens the risk is beneficial to the underwriter.

    Contracts of indemnity

    Insurance contracts are contracts of indemnity under which the insurer agrees to make good the insured’s loss by payment, repair, reinstatement or some other method. The principle of indemnity is that the insured should be neither better nor worse off after the occurrence of an insured event but (in so far as a sum of money can do so) should be placed in exactly the same position as if the event insured against had not happened. If property worth £500 is lost then the insurer pays £500 to the insured. The insured is thus indemnified. Even if insured for £900 the insured still gets £500. To give the insured more than the loss is to encourage the insured to bring about the insured event. The onus of seeing that the sum insured is correct is on the insured.

    Subrogation and contribution

    The matter of indemnity is subject to two important principles known as subrogation and contribution.

    Subrogation means that where an insured has been indemnified, then the insurer can stand in the insured’s shoes and avail himself of all rights and remedies open to the insured. In other words, if the isnsured’s property worth £500 has been damaged by negligence of a third party, the insurer will pay £500 to the insured. The insured will have been indemnified. By subrogation the insurer is then entitled, in the insured’s name, to sue the third party to recover the £500.

    Contribution applies where the same interest and the same risk are insured by more than one policy. In that case both policies contribute towards the loss in accordance with certain legal principles. The insured cannot recover the loss in full under each of the policies, as that would defeat the principle of indemnity.

    Joint insurance and co-insurance

    Joint insurance arises where two or more insureds have the same interest in the subject matter of the insurance, for example joint owners of a piece of land or land and buildings.

    Co-insurance arises where two or more insureds have different interests in the same property, for example it has been decided by the courts, in Petrofina v. Magnaload (1983) that a main contractor and sub-contractor are co-insureds. The significance is that in joint insurance a breach of duty by one joint insured will entitle an insurer to avoid the policy against all joint insureds, whereas a breach of duty by one co-insured entities an insurer to avoid the policy only against the co-insured in breach; the policy remains valid and subsisting for co-insured not in breach of duty.

    It should be noted that reference later in this text to a policy being ‘in the joint names of a Contractor and Employer’ is a reference to co-insurance and not joint insurance, as their respective interests in the subject matter of the insurance are different.

    Layout of a typical insurance policy

    A typical insurance policy consists principally of policy wording and a policy schedule. Detailed provisions are dealt with in later chapters dealing with specific types of policy.

    The wording is what one might call the ‘standard terms’ of the insurance, although in practice it is not uncommon for the wording to be specially drafted or tailored to suit the needs of a particular insured. Where the wording is not the ‘standard’ product of an insurer or insurance broker, it is often referred to as a ‘bespoke wording’.

    The wording is likely to include the following:

    • The insuring clause: the words used to describe the nature of the insurance. In the typical Public Liability policy in Appendix 1 and Employer’s Liability policy in Appendix 2, the insuring clause is in Section 2. In the typical Contract Works policy in Appendix 3, it is in Section 1. The insuring clause often shows with whom lies the burden of proof that a valid claim exists: in all ‘all-risks’ policy (e.g. Appendix 3 — referring to ‘loss or damage however caused’) the insured need prove only unintended damage. The burden of proof then shifts to the insurer to prove an exclusion or other provision which may exclude or limit the insurer’s liability. In contrast, the insuring clauses in the typical policies in Appendices 1 and 2 describe the subject of the policy cover, and the initial burden of proof is upon the insured to show that the claim is within the description in the insuring clause.
    • Extensions: these are additions to the basic cover provided by a policy and may be referred to as ‘memoranda’, ‘endorsements’ (as in Appendix 1), or simply as ‘extensions’ (as in Appendix 3).
    • Exclusions (also called ‘exceptions’): examples appear in the typical policies. The burden of proof is upon an insurer to establish that a risk or claim is excluded by this type of provision.
    • Warranties: the typical policies given in the Appendices do not include examples of express warranties. In these types of policy a warranty will usually relate to the conduct of an insured when undertaking a particular task. For example, a contractor who often uses flame-cutting equipment may be required to warrant that when so doing there will be fire extinguishers within easy reach, a fire-watch will be maintained and the area will be checked for combustible materials before work begins. As already mentioned,warranties have particular significance in insurance law and must be strictly complied with.
    • Conditions: there are three basic kinds of policy condition:
      • A condition precedent to the policy, breach of which renders the policy voidable ab initio at the discretion of the insurer. An example would be a breach of the duty of utmost good faith, such as a failure to disclose material information.
      • A condition subsequent to the policy may render the policy voidable from the date of the breach. An example would be a change in the risk so that it is different from that which the underwriter thought he was covering. The policy is effective for all claims up to the date of the change but not thereafter.
      • A condition precedent to liability is one which must be observed before the insurer becomes liable under the policy. This kind of condition does not affect the validity of the policy but may affect the right of an insured to indemnity for a particular claim. For example, a condition requiring a contractor to notify claims within a stated time, if breached, may entitle an insurer to reject a claim.

    The schedule of a policy will contain details specific to the subject being insured. The typical policies outlined in the Appendices provide examples of the details given in such schedules.
