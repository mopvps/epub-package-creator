5 A Contractor’s Public Liability policy

The liability of a Contractor for causing injury to third parties or damage to third party property is covered by the Contractor’s Public Liability policy (sometimes called a Third Party policy).

Policies vary from insurer to insurer. Some specialise in contractor’s insurances, others do not. In practice there is much variation in the cover given by policies and there is no such thing as a standard Public Liability policy.

A specimen policy is included in Appendix 1. It is intended to be typical of the type of policy which is met in practice. For ease of reference, the various parts of the policy have been given numbered section headings in the Appendix and these section numbers are referred to in the following text.

Section 1 – The opening clause

This is a typical opening clause to an insurance policy. There are a number of important points to note.

  • The premium charged by an insurer and the extent of cover an insurer is prepared to give are geared to the business to be insured. It is important, therefore, to see that the business is adequately described. A policy describing the insured simply as a building contractor, for example, would not insure against liabilities arising during civil engineering work on bridges. A policy showing the insured as a painting contractor would not insure against liabilities arising during demolition work.
  • An insurer obtains underwriting information (the basis for the decision whether or not to accept the risk and the amount of premium) from information supplied by or on behalf of an insured in proposing the risk. The proposal, which may include a proposal form if required by the insurer, will almost certainly include information about the contractor’s business in the case of annual covers or about the proposed works in the case of project cover. Any inaccuracies in the proposal may give an insurer a right to avoid the policy. Hence the proposal form and any other information should be completed with great care and a copy of it always retained for future reference.
  • The premium is the contract ‘consideration’ for which the insurer agrees to enter into the insurance contract and to issue the policy.

Section 2 – The insuring clause

This is the insuring clause of the policy setting out the cover. The following are the important points to note.

  • The cover is subject to the other terms, exceptions, limits and conditions set out in the policy. In other words, the document has to be read as a whole.
  • The insurer agrees to indemnify the insured against the insured’s legal liability. In other words, the insurer will stand in the insured’s shoes, negotiate on the insured’s behalf and pay any damages as may be necessary. The insured is usually a limited liability company or partnership. Many policies extend the definition to include directors and employees of the insured so that they too are protected by the policy for their activities in connection with the business.
  • The cover is to pay all sums which the insured shall become legally liable to pay. As explained in Chapter 2, legal liability can arise in many circumstances including negligence, nuisance, trespass, strict liability, liability under statute and contractual liability. It is a legal liability policy, not one covering moral liabilities or liabilities voluntarily incurred for business or other reasons.
  • The promise is to pay any damages awarded against the insured, i.e. the amount of compensation to which the claimant is entitled. (In practice most claims are settled by negotiation. Few go to court.)
  • The damages must relate to a claimant’s injury or damage and must be awarded ‘in respect of that injury or damage. Thus the policy will protect the insured against the insured’s liability for injury or damage sustained directly by the claimant plus any consequential losses which may flow from that injury or damage. In other words, if a claimant’s house is badly damaged and the claimant incurs costs in staying in an hotel while the repairs are carried out, the policy will pay for the damage to the house plus the necessary hotel expenses and any other consequential losses flowing from the damage.
  • The policy protects against liability for bodily injury to third parties which includes death, illness, accident and disease.
  • The policy protects against liability in respect of loss or damage to property and losses consequent upon such damage. The term ‘property’ is capable of a wide meaning and can include both tangible and intangible property. The intention of most insurers is to cover only damage to tangible property and liability for interference with the rights of others by, for instance, obstructing the highway, trespassing, causing excessive dust or noise, restricting use or enjoyment of rights of way, light and water. What the policy is not intended to cover is loss of or damage to intellectual property (patents, copyright, designs, etc.) and it may contain an express exclusion to that effect.
  • The policy operates only if the injury or damage is accidental. Injury or damage which is inevitable, unavoidable or deliberate is not covered. The intention is to construe the word ‘accidental’ from the insured’s point of view which, in the case of a limited liability company, means the viewpoint of the directors and senior management. It is not the intention to construe the word from an employee’s point of view; for example, reckless conduct by an employee may result in damage which, although not accidental from his or her point of view, is from that of the employer. In practice, difficulties can arise in interpretation.

    • Injury or damage is accidental when it is the unexpected or unlooked-for consequence of an untoward event which is not expected or designed. In other words it must be fortuitous, as for example when an excavator damages an underground cable because the operator did not know it was there. But if the insured knew its location and took no precautions, the damage would not be accidental. The inevitable consequences of damage by vibration during pile driving, concussion damage arising out of the use of explosives, and dust arising from demolition sites are not accidental.

    In some policies the word ‘accidental’ may be omitted by insurers. Nevertheless the intention of the insurers is only to pay for fortuitous damage and one will find an exclusion elsewhere in the policy excluding damage which arises out of the deliberate acts or omissions of the insured or which is inevitable or unavoidable (several different wordings are in use).

  • The liability and the injury, loss or damage must arise in connection with the business covered by the policy – hence the importance of describing it accurately.
  • The injury, loss or damage must occur within the territorial limits covered by the policy, e.g. in the United Kingdom. Where work is performed abroad, a special extension to the policy will be required.
  • The injury, loss or damage must occur during the period of insurance. Once the policy has lapsed there is no cover, even though the events giving rise to the subsequent injury, loss or damage may have occurred during the period of insurance. Thus if structures erected negligently during the period of insurance subsequently collapse and injure a third party after the policy has lapsed, any claim then made will not be covered, because at the date the third party was injured the policy was not in force. Hence a contractor who arranges individual policies for each contract and allows them to lapse when the contract has been finished runs the risk of being uninsured for subsequent injury or damage. If a contractor has an annual policy it is sensible to arrange with insurers to extend that policy to cover this contingent risk.

Section 3 – The limit of indemnity

There is a limit given in a policy up to which the insurer will pay claims, but not beyond. This is known as the limit of indemnity. It is usually a limit per ‘occurrence’. It is important that the insurer describes exactly in what circumstances the limit of indemnity will operate because a great deal is at risk. Thus if there is an ‘occurrence’ (such as an explosion), which injures a great many people, the insurer will not pay more than the limit of indemnity, no matter how many claimants there may be. If the limit is £1 million, that represents the maximum exposure. It is £1 million for the ‘occurrence’, not £1 million per claimant.

Sometimes a lower limit of indemnity may apply to certain parts of the policy, e.g. for damage arising out of fire or explosion or for damage to property arising out of vibration or the removal or weakening of support.

A limit of indemnity may also be exposed ‘in the aggregate’ to create an overall limit on the insurer’s exposure in respect of all occurrences during the period of insurance.

The insurer will also pay the legal costs and expenses of litigation incurred in negotiating or defending a claim. In practice these often amount to a substantial amount and are usually paid in addition to the limit of indemnity. In other words, if the limit of indemnity is £1 million and a plaintiffs claim is settled for £1 million, the insurer will in addition pay the legal costs incurred in negotiating settlement of the claim.

Section 4 – Exceptions

Policy exceptions appear frequently in insurance policies for a number of reasons. Some risks may be insured under other policies and are therefore excluded; otherwise there would be a duplication in cover and unnecessary overpayment of premium. Some risks are excluded because the insurer is not prepared to cover them at all, or unless there is further underwriting information. Other risks are excluded because they are not insurable in the ordinary insurance market, e.g. liability arising out of ionising radiations or contamination by radioactivity from any nuclear fuel.

Using the same numbering as that in the specimen policy in Appendix 1, the following brief comments explains why the exclusions appear and what they mean.

  1. Injury to the categories of persons mentioned is excluded because this risk is usually and more properly insured under an Employers’ Liability policy.
  2. Property belonging to the insured is excluded from a Public Liability policy because such property should be insured under a separate Material Damage policy. For instance, if a contractor has loaned property to a third party and then negligently damages the property while it is still in the custody of the third party, the contractor cannot claim under its own Public Liability policy for damage to its own property.

    3. Third party property in the insured’s charge or control is excluded because insurers take the view that it too should be insured under a Material Damage policy. In practice a great deal of discussion takes place on the wording of this particular exception and amendments are made. However, the exception does make it clear that when a contractor is working in a third party’s premises, those premises and their contents are to be regarded as not being in the contractor’s charge or control for the purposes of activating the exception.

  3. Passenger lifts, aircraft, marine vessels, vehicles and plant are excluded because they should be insured under separate policies.
  4. Traditionally the making good by a contractor of defective site work or materials is regarded by insurers as a trade risk and is therefore excluded: the policy is not intended to be a guarantee as to the skill or competence of the contractor’s employees.
  5. Contractual liability, i.e. liability assumed under agreement, is often an excluded risk under Liability Insurance policies. The reason for this is that while an insurer can generally assess from its own information and experience the risk of injury or damage arising out of tort, it is less easy when liability attaches under contractual agreements. In the United Kingdom, contracting parties are free to contract in whatever terms they wish and liability is frequently transferred from one person to another. A contractor may, therefore, assume very onerous responsibilities which require specific declaration to insure and, if accepted, special underwriting.

    6. This exception is, therefore, often qualified by the words in brackets ‘(other than as defined in Endorsement 1)’ to enable a contractor to negotiate with insurers to include some or all aspects of liability assumed under the construction contract. The point will be explained in greater detail when Endorsement 1 is considered. In some policies the exception does not appear.

  6. The object of the pollution provision is to keep out of cover the potentially very substantial clean-up costs and related liabilities for death or injury caused over a period of years as a result of what are considered by insurers to be trade risks or a lack of care by producers of waste.
  7. This exception appears because it deals with risks which are not insurable in the commercial insurance market.
  8. Like exception 7, exception 8 deals with risks which are not insurable in the commercial insurance market.

Section 5– Endorsements

Endorsement 1

This Endorsement overrides and amends the exception dealing with liability under agreement. Where the contractor has entered into an agreement with a principal, i.e. the Employer under ICE Conditions of Contract, then the policy will protect the contractor in respect of the contractual liability assumed but only so far as concerns injury to third parties or damage to third party property. The policy thus indemnifies a contractor against limited liability under, for example, Clause 22(1) of ICE 6, subject always to the other terms and exceptions of the policy. However, the policy will not pay for the cost of making good defective work or materials, or damage to structures erected by the insured, since these are still excluded perils. Nor will the policy pay for liquidated damages arising from delay. These are not risks covered by the policy in the first place.

Acontractor may enter into other agreements where contractual liability is assumed, e.g. hire of plant. Such liability would not be covered by the above policy since the plant owner is not the principal for whom the contractor has agreed to carry out work.

Endorsement 2

The policy is in the name of the insured – typically in contracts which are subject to ICE Conditions, the Contractor. To comply with ICE Conditions of Contract, which require the insurance also to indemnify the principal (the Employer), Endorsement 2 appears. Thus the policy grants an indemnity to the Employer and complies with, for example, Clause 23(1) of ICE 6.

Section 6– The schedule

The description of ‘the Business’ must be accurate. It must embrace all the activities in which a contractor is engaged. If work is done outside those business activities the contractor is uninsured. For example, in one claim a tunnelling contractor was working near a factory. The factory owner urgently required some equipment in his factory to be cut out and removed. The contractor agreed to do it but caused a fire resulting in substantial damage and was sued. The contractor’s Public Liability policy did not provide protection because the work did not fall within the definition of work normally carried out by tunnelling contractors.

Other amendments that may appear to the policy

The specimen policy in Appendix 1 is meant to be a typically average Public Liability policy. In practice a number of other limitations may apply.

Damage to property

The policy may exclude damage to property arising out of the removal or weakening of support, or alternatively will grant the cover but subject to a low limit of indemnity.

Professional negligence

The policy may exclude what the insurer considers to be a ‘professional negligence’ risk on the basis that this should be insured under a Professional Indemnity policy. Thus the policy may exclude injury or damage arising out of faulty advice, design or specification, or breach of professional duty.

Hazardous work

Certain forms of ‘hazardous’ work may be excluded. This may be on the basis that the insurer does not wish to cover it, or on the basis that the contractor does not wish to pay the additional premium to include it. The policy may, therefore, exclude work in connection with the use of explosives, tunnelling or demolition.

Pure financial loss

It will be remembered that the policy only operates if there is personal injury or damage to property. In those events the policy will provide indemnity against the liability for the injury or damage and the financial loss arising as a result of such injury or damage. What the policy will not do, however, is to provide indemnity where the liability is for pure economic loss.

It is useful to consider what is meant by pure economic loss, the circumstances where it is likely to be insured by the Public Liability policy and the extent to which it needs to be insured having regard to the potential liability of the contractor therefor.

What is pure economic loss? It will certainly include the loss arising in the event of infringement of intellectual property rights such as breach of copyright or patent, and awards in respect of defamation (libel and slander). It will also include losses which arise without physical damage to the tangible property of the person who suffers the loss.

There are essentially three ways in which liability for economic loss might arise:

  • as a result of a breach of contract;
  • as a result of negligence; and
  • as a result of the other torts mentioned in item 3 of Section 2 of the policy.

The policy will cover liability in respect of the last-mentioned type of claim. Unless the policy is extended it will not cover liability for pure economic loss arising as a result of breach of contract or negligence.

Pure economic loss is recoverable as damages for breach of contract provided that it falls within the rule in Hadley v. Baxendale (1854). That which is recoverable is (1) the loss which may fairly and reasonably be regarded as arising naturally, that is to say according to the usual course of things, from the breach, or (2) the loss which ought to have been foreseen, or in the contemplation of the parties, at the time the contract was made.

The position in negligence is more difficult. Except where there is a negligent mis-statement within the meaning of Hedley Byrne v. Heller & Partners (1963), pure economic loss is not recoverable. That which is regarded in law as pure economic loss may be divided into two types.

The first is exemplified by the decision of the House of Lords in D&F Estates Limited v. Church Commissioners (1989). An apartment block had been built by a main contractor for a developer. The plasterwork to the interior of the apartments had been carelessly carried out by a domestic sub-contractor such that the plaster was defective and required replacement. The tenants of the apartments sued the main contractor in negligence for damages representing the cost of removing and replacing the plaster. There was no personal injury or damage to property save for damage to carpets and decor. The claim was held to be for pure economic loss which was not recoverable. The Court went on to say that the principle established by Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932) was that a manufacturer of a product is liable if, due to negligent manufacture, the product causes injury or damage to property, but that damage to the product itself was not included and was recoverable only by one contracting party against another. The principle was applied, by analogy, to buildings. The tenants were unable to recover damages from the main contractor and, on the facts, were unable to recover the damage to carpets and decor because the main contractor was not liable for the errors of the plastering sub-contractor. The main contractor’s only duty in that regard was to employ a competent sub-contractor – which he had done.

The second type of case is where the claimant has nothing to do with the works or the finished product but is affected by the carrying out of the works. A good example of the law relating to recovery of pure economic loss is the case of Spartan Steel & Alloys Limited v. Martin & Co. (Contractors) Limited (1973). The facts, in brief, are that Spartan were aluminium smelters and producers whose plant was run by electricity supplied through cables belonging to the electricity supply authority for the area. Martin damaged the cables so that the power supply to Spartan ’s factory was interrupted. Spartan claimed (i) the value of material which had solidified in the plant and the cost of breaking it out; (ii) loss of profit on that material; and (iii) further loss of profit due to the downtime caused by the lack of electricity. Spartan were entitled to recover (i) and (ii) but not (iii), which was pure economic loss unconnected with damage to Spartan’s property.

When financial loss cover (the insurance term for the only cover available for pure economic loss) is granted as an extension to a Public Liability policy it is subject to many limitations, e.g. it may exclude the cost of repairing or replacing defective work, failure of a product to fulfil its intended function, breach of professional duty, infringement of plans, copyrights, patents, designs and liquidated damages. Moreover, not all insurers are prepared to provide this cover.

An example of a typical financial loss wording is as follows.

‘The company shall subject to the terms and conditions of this Policy indemnify the Insured against:

Liability for accidental Financial Loss occurring during the period of insurance (not occasioned by loss of or damage to Property)

The indemnity granted by this Extension shall not apply in respect of

  1. (a) the first 10% or £xx whichever is the greater of the damages costs or claimant’s expenses which shall be retained by the Insured as his own liability and uninsured
  2. (b) any liability which is assumed by the insured by agreement (other than liability arising out of a condition or warranty implied by law) unless such liability would have attached in the absence of such agreement
  3. (c)any liability arising from a breach of professional duty
  4. (d)passing off or infringement of patent copyright design trademark or trade name
  5. (e)any liability for a breach of obligation owed by the insured as an employer to an Employee
  6. (f)the cost of repairing replacing or recalling any faulty product or material’.

Sub-Contractors

The Insured’s vicarious liability arising out of the use of subcontractors is covered but the policy gives no direct indemnity to the sub-contractors themselves. They are responsible for arranging their own insurance. However, the position is negotiable and where it is necessary for special contracts to meet the requirements of contract conditions, the policy can be extended by negotiation to include an indemnity to the subcontractors themselves.

Policy conditions

Policy conditions have already been considered in principle in Chapter 1. Here the particular conditions in the specimen policy in Appendix 1 are considered.

  • A requirement that immediate notification is given to insurers of any injury or damage or any claim. The object is to give an insurer the chance to investigate while the evidence is still fresh.
  • A condition prohibiting an insured from negotiating any claim or repudiating liability or admitting liability. This is on the basis that if the Insurer is going to pay, the Insurer has the right to decide those matters.
  • Where a claim exceeds the limit of indemnity selected by the Contractor, the Insurer has a right to apply that limit to the Contractor’s claim and thereafter withdraw from the claim. In other words, if the limit of indemnity is £1 million and a claim against the Contractor is for £10 million the Insurer can give the contractor £1 million, pay the costs incurred so far and thereafter leave negotiation of the claim with the Contractor. The insurer then has no further involvement.
  • A condition that where there is more than one policy in force (as there often is), each policy shall contribute according to a formula; for example, if there are two similar policies, each may pay half of the claim.
  • A condition requiring the Insured to exercise reasonable care in the conduct of business to prevent injury, loss or damage. The House of Lords has, however, decided (in Sofi v. Prudential Assurance) that before insurers can invoke the condition they have to show that the Insured’s, i.e. the director or senior management’s, conduct was reckless – in other words that the Insured acted with actual knowledge and recognition that a danger existed but not caring whether or not it was averted.
  • A condition requiring that if the risk changes the Insured must give notice in writing to the Insurer.
  • A condition giving the Insurer the right to cancel the policy by sending 30 days’ notice by registered letter to the Insured’s last known address.
